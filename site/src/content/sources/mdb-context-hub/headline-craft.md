# Headline Craft

## Overview

Five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy. A headline does three jobs at once: it tells the reader what they're about to get, gives them a reason to care, and signals what kind of piece it is.

## Core Concepts

### 1. The five-times rule and the 80-cent budget

Ogilvy's empirical claim: five times more people read the headline than the body. The practical implication: the headline is where 80% of the work happens. Joanna Wiebe's discipline: never write fewer than 10 headline variants before picking one. Ogilvy demanded 16.

### 2. AIDA mapped onto a headline

| AIDA stage | Where it lives | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Attention** | First 3 words of the headline | Stops the scroll. |
| **Interest** | Rest of the headline | Promises a benefit, names a tension, or opens a curiosity gap. |
| **Desire** | Subhead / deck | Adds the second beat — specificity, proof, or who-it's-for. |
| **Action** | First line of body / CTA | Closes the loop the headline opened. |

### 3. The "you-frame" rule

A headline pointed at the reader outperforms a headline pointed at the writer, the product, or an abstract topic.

- **Topic frame (weak):** "The future of distributed databases"
- **You-frame (strong):** "What your replication setup is costing you (and how to fix it)"

### 4. Numbers in headlines: odd, specific, and not too round

BuzzSumo's analysis of 100M headlines: numerals increase CTR by ~36%.

- **B2C top performers:** 10, 5, 15, 7, 20, 6
- **Odd numbers** outperform even numbers for curiosity-driven content.
- **Specific numbers** outperform round numbers. "$1,247 saved" beats "$1,200 saved."

### 5. Curiosity-gap vs declarative headlines

**Declarative headlines** state the conclusion in the headline itself. Good for hard news, technical posts.

**Curiosity-gap headlines** withhold the conclusion to drive the click. The failure mode: **clickbait** — a gap so wide the body can't close it.

### 6. Channel-specific length budgets

| Channel | Sweet spot | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Web article H1** | 8–12 words | |
| **SEO title tag** | 50–60 chars | Google truncates beyond pixel width |
| **X / Twitter** | 60–70 chars | Under 70 chars lets retweeters add commentary |
| **Email subject line** | 30–50 chars | iPhone portrait shows ~35–40 chars |
| **Push notification** | 25–40 chars | Verb + benefit only |

### 7. News vs feature vs op-ed vs SEO headline registers

- **News headline:** Active verb, present tense. "Atlas adds vector search for Voyage embeddings."
- **Feature headline:** Label or question form OK. "The quiet revolution in how databases handle vectors."
- **Op-ed headline:** Argument-forward. "We're thinking about vector search wrong."
- **SEO headline:** Keyword-front-loaded. "Vector search benchmarks: 12 databases compared (2026)."

### 8. The deck / subhead pairing

A deck does three things:
1. Expands what the headline could not fit.
2. Answers the question the headline raised.
3. Earns the scroll.

Sizing: Heds are 6–10 words. Decks are 10–25 words.

## Example rewrites

| Weak | Stronger | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "The future of databases" | "What your database will look like in 2027 (and why it's not what you think)" | You-frame + curiosity gap |
| "MongoDB launches Search Nodes" | "Search Nodes cut Atlas query latency 4x in production" | Verb-led, evidence-led, benefit-front |
| "10 things to know about indexes" | "9 indexing mistakes I found in 200 production clusters" | Odd number, specific evidence |

## Anti-Patterns

- **The "we" headline.** "We released a new feature today." The reader doesn't care what *you* did.
- **The label headline.** "Distributed Systems Notes" names a folder, doesn't earn a click.
- **The pun headline.** Ogilvy banned them in 1963 and the data still says he's right.
- **The bait-and-switch.** Headline promises X, body delivers Y.
- **The under-specified curiosity gap.** "What we learned" without a hint of *what* you learned.
