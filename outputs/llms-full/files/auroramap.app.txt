## Purpose

AuroraMap.app helps users understand whether aurora may be visible, what current space-weather signals mean, and how to plan a viewing session. The site is designed for aurora enthusiasts, photographers, travelers, educators, and sky watchers.

The site does not replace official space-weather services. It organizes public space-weather and weather data into a user-friendly forecast dashboard and educational guide. When accuracy matters, users should verify current warnings and alerts with official sources such as NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center.

### Cloud Layers And Sky Cameras

The default map cloud layer uses OpenWeather Weather Maps 1.0 `clouds_new`. It is worldwide modeled current cloud cover intended to support viewing decisions. It is not direct satellite imagery and does not guarantee conditions at a particular ground location.

The optional NASA map layer uses the previous UTC day's Terra and Aqua MODIS night infrared cloud-phase observations from NASA EOSDIS GIBS. The site renders the scientific layer in neutral gray so it can be compared with the aurora oval. It is an observational satellite product with possible swath gaps and processing delay, not a current weather forecast.

Camera markers and the crawlable source list link directly to operator pages:

- Poker Flat, Alaska: University of Alaska Fairbanks Geophysical Institute, https://allsky.gi.alaska.edu/
- Yellowknife, Canada: AuroraMAX, University of Calgary and Canadian Space Agency, https://auroramax.com/live
- Kiruna, Sweden: Swedish Institute of Space Physics, https://www.irf.se/en/forskning/kago/firmamentkamera/
- Queenstown, New Zealand: Roundshot Queenstown, https://queenstown.roundshot.com/#/

These are third-party sources. Availability depends on local darkness, season, weather, maintenance and operator policy. Some sources publish frequently refreshed still images rather than continuous video. A camera image alone does not establish that an aurora is visible at that moment.

## Core Concepts

### Aurora Borealis And Aurora Australis

Aurora appears when charged particles from the solar wind interact with Earth's magnetosphere and upper atmosphere. Particles are guided toward polar regions by Earth's magnetic field. When those particles collide with oxygen and nitrogen, they emit visible light.

- Aurora Borealis: northern lights, usually visible at high northern latitudes.
- Aurora Australis: southern lights, usually visible at high southern latitudes.
- Green aurora often comes from oxygen emissions around 100-300 km altitude.
- Red aurora often comes from higher-altitude oxygen emissions.
- Purple, blue, and pink tones often involve nitrogen emissions or mixed emissions.

### KP Index

KP is a global geomagnetic storm index from 0 to 9. Higher values usually mean stronger geomagnetic activity and a wider aurora oval.

General viewing interpretation:

- KP 0-2: Usually limited to high-latitude aurora zones.
- KP 3-4: Possible at mid-high latitudes under dark, clear skies.
- KP 5-6: Geomagnetic storm conditions; aurora may expand to lower latitudes.
- KP 7-9: Strong to extreme storm levels; aurora can appear unusually far from polar regions.

KP is not a guarantee of visibility. Cloud cover, daylight, moonlight, local light pollution, and exact magnetic latitude all matter.

### Bz Magnetic Field

Bz is the north-south component of the interplanetary magnetic field. Sustained negative Bz is often favorable for aurora because it can couple more effectively with Earth's magnetic field and allow solar wind energy into the magnetosphere.

General interpretation:

- Positive Bz: usually less favorable for aurora.
- Mildly negative Bz: can support activity, especially at high latitudes.
- Strong sustained negative Bz: can support stronger aurora, especially when paired with elevated solar wind speed and density.

### Solar Wind

Solar wind is a stream of charged particles from the Sun. The dashboard uses solar wind context to help users understand whether conditions are becoming more favorable or less favorable for aurora.

Important signals include:

- Speed: faster solar wind can increase geomagnetic activity.
- Density: higher particle density can increase energy input.
- Magnetic field direction: Bz direction is especially important.
- Timing: solar wind measured upstream of Earth can arrive after a delay.

### Hemispheric Power Index

HPI estimates auroral energy input in gigawatts. Higher values usually indicate stronger auroral activity across a hemisphere. HPI should be interpreted with KP, Bz, location, darkness, and weather.

### Coronal Mass Ejections

A Coronal Mass Ejection is a large solar eruption that can disturb Earth's magnetosphere if Earth-directed. CMEs can produce major geomagnetic storms, but timing and intensity are uncertain until the solar wind disturbance is observed closer to Earth.

### Moon Phase And Weather

Aurora can be present but not visible to a user if local viewing conditions are poor.

Key constraints:

- Cloud cover can completely block aurora.
- High moon illumination can wash out faint aurora.
- City light pollution reduces contrast.
- Local midnight and dark hours are usually better than twilight.
- A rural dark-sky location improves visibility.

## Practical User Workflows

### Check Whether Aurora Is Possible Tonight

1. Open the dashboard.
2. Check current KP and the near-term KP outlook.
3. Check Bz and solar wind context.
4. Check aurora map probability near the user's region.
5. Check cloud cover, moon phase, and local darkness.
6. Pick a dark location with a clear view toward the relevant horizon.

### Plan A Northern Hemisphere Viewing Session

For Aurora Borealis:

- High-latitude locations have the best odds.
- During stronger storms, aurora can expand toward mid-latitudes.
- Northern horizon visibility matters for lower-latitude observers.
- Local cloud cover can be more important than a high KP value.

Common regions include Alaska, northern Canada, Iceland, northern Scandinavia, northern Scotland, and high-latitude parts of Russia. During strong storms, northern U.S. states and parts of central/northern Europe may have chances.

### Plan A Southern Hemisphere Viewing Session

For Aurora Australis:

- Tasmania and southern New Zealand are important accessible viewing regions.
- Southern Chile, southern Argentina, and subantarctic regions can have strong viewing potential.
- During major storms, aurora may be visible farther north than usual.
- A clear southern horizon is important for many observers.

### Photograph Aurora

Basic starting settings:

- Use a tripod.
- Use manual focus set carefully on distant stars or infinity.
- Use a wide-angle lens when available.
- Start around ISO 1600-3200.
- Start around f/2.8 or wider if possible.
- Use exposure times around 5-15 seconds for moderate activity.
- Shorten exposure when aurora moves quickly or is very bright.
- Shoot RAW when possible.

These settings are starting points. Users should adjust for camera model, lens, aurora brightness, moonlight, and foreground exposure.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I see the northern lights tonight?

Visibility depends on the KP index (geomagnetic activity), your latitude, cloud cover, and sky darkness. Check the live KP forecast and aurora probability map, confirm clear skies, and find a dark location facing the poleward horizon. A strong forecast can still be defeated by clouds or a bright moon.

### What KP index do I need to see the aurora?

It depends on latitude: KP 1-2 above the Arctic Circle (60-70°N), KP 3+ around 55-60°N (Fairbanks, Reykjavik, Tromso), KP 5+ at 50-55°N (Glasgow, Stockholm), and KP 7+ at 40-50°N (New York, London, Paris). KP is a probability signal, not a guarantee.

### Where are the best places to see the northern lights?

Inside the auroral oval at roughly 65-75° magnetic latitude. Accessible top spots: Tromso (Norway), Abisko (Sweden), Reykjavik (Iceland), Fairbanks (Alaska), Yellowknife (Canada). For aurora australis: Tasmania, southern New Zealand, southern Chile.

### What time of night is best?

Around local magnetic midnight, typically 22:00-02:00 local time, under full darkness away from city lights.

### Does the moon affect visibility?

Yes. A bright or full moon washes out faint aurora. Viewing is best near the new moon.

### What is Bz and why does it matter?

Bz is the north-south component of the interplanetary magnetic field. Sustained negative (southward) Bz, especially below -10 nT, is favorable for aurora because it couples with Earth's magnetic field and lets in solar wind energy.

### What is the difference between aurora borealis and aurora australis?

Same phenomenon at opposite poles: borealis in the high north, australis in the high south. Both are driven by the same space-weather conditions.

### Is Aurora Map free?

Yes. AuroraMap.app is a free web app with free iOS and Android companion apps. Data comes from NOAA SWPC and Open-Meteo.
