## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best AI writing tool in 2026?

EVY is the top-rated AI writing tool for voice-first workflows at $19/month — it accepts voice input, generates complete drafts in your brand voice, and runs inside any Mac application. ChatGPT ($20/month) remains the best for brainstorming and research. Jasper ($69/month) suits enterprise teams needing structured templates.

### Is EVY better than ChatGPT for content creation?

For ongoing content production, EVY outperforms ChatGPT in three key areas: voice input (EVY writes from spoken ideas; ChatGPT requires typed prompts), brand voice consistency (EVY learns your style once and maintains it; ChatGPT forgets every session), and content organisation (EVY has a built-in library; ChatGPT has chat history). ChatGPT is better for one-off research, coding, and open-ended conversation.

### Which AI writing tool supports voice input?

EVY and Wispr Flow both support voice input, but they work differently. Wispr Flow transcribes your speech verbatim — fast and accurate, but you still write and edit the content yourself. EVY takes your spoken ideas and writes finished, structured content in your brand voice. For content creation, EVY is the clear choice; for quick dictation across any app, Wispr Flow is excellent.

### Can AI writing tools learn my brand voice?

EVY and Jasper both offer brand voice training. EVY learns from 3–5 uploaded content samples and applies your voice automatically to every piece — no re-prompting required. Jasper's brand voice requires setup through their interface and must be selected per template. ChatGPT, Notion AI, Granola, and Wispr Flow do not offer persistent brand voice learning.

### What is the best AI writing tool for macOS?

EVY is built specifically for macOS and integrates at the operating system level — its AI works inside any app you already use (Google Docs, Notion, email, Slack) without switching tabs. Granola and Wispr Flow also offer native macOS apps. ChatGPT and Jasper are web-based and run in any browser on Mac.

### How much do AI writing tools cost?

AI writing tools range from free to $69+/month. EVY offers a free tier (1,000 credits) and a Plus plan at $19/month. ChatGPT Plus is $20/month. Notion AI is ~$10/month as an add-on. Wispr Flow is $15/month. Granola starts free with paid plans around $18/month. Jasper starts at $69/month for individuals.

### What is the difference between AI writing and AI dictation?

AI dictation (like Wispr Flow) transcribes your voice into text — you speak, it types. AI writing (like EVY) takes your spoken or typed ideas and produces structured, edited, publish-ready content. The difference: dictation replaces your keyboard; AI writing replaces the drafting and editing process. For polished, on-brand content, AI writing saves significantly more time.

## Five mechanics that decide whether anyone sees your post

This is where most "I tried LinkedIn and it didn't work" consultants lost. Their ideas were fine. They lost on the five things below.

1.  1
    
    ### Write the hook last.
    
    Your first line has one job: earn the second line. Most people pour their energy into the body and bolt a warm-up sentence on top. Backwards. Write the whole post first, then hunt for the most surprising sentence in it (usually buried in the middle, the admission or the number or the line that made you slightly nervous to type), cut it, and move it to the top. A good hook lands cold, with zero context. "I lost a $400K client over a typo" works. "Some thoughts on client communication" does not.
    
2.  2
    
    ### Keep links out of the post.
    
    LinkedIn quietly buries posts that try to send people off the platform. The best creators know this, so they post clean and drop the link in the first comment instead, with a "link in the comments" at the end of the post. Costs you nothing, and it protects your reach.
    
3.  3
    
    ### The first 90 minutes decide everything.
    
    The algorithm shows every post to a small group first. Engage fast and it goes wide. Stay quiet and it dies. So post when your buyers are actually awake (Tuesday to Thursday, 7 to 9am their time, for most consulting audiences), then stay for an hour and reply to every comment the moment it lands. Posting and walking away is how good posts die at 200 views.
    
4.  4
    
    ### Comments are worth more than likes.
    
    A like is a nod. A comment is a conversation, and conversations turn into calls. The algorithm rewards them far more heavily too. So build the post to provoke one: end on a real question, or a take people will want to argue with. Agreeable posts get scrolled past.
    
5.  5
    
    ### Write for a thumb.
    
    Your reader is on a phone, in a queue, half paying attention. One idea per post. Short lines. A blank line between them. Nothing longer than two lines in a row. A wall of text is a signal to scroll, and the algorithm watches how fast they do.
    

## What to post, and how often

Knowing the mechanics isn't the same as knowing what to say. Here's a mix that holds up on both LinkedIn and X, week after week.

-   **Authority, 50%.** Specific insights, frameworks, and hard-won lessons from the actual work. This is what makes someone trust you with their problem.
-   **Personal, 25%.** A real story with a business lesson, or why you do this work. Trust is built here, more than anywhere else.
-   **Build in public, 15%.** What you're working on, what's landing, what isn't. An ongoing story people come back to follow.
-   **Offers, 10%.** A direct ask: a client result, open capacity, what you sell. You earn this slice with the other 90%.

**How often:** three to five posts a week, every week. Consistency beats volume. A steady three a week for six months will out-earn ten in a launch week followed by silence. Pick a cadence you can hold when client work gets heavy, because that's the only test that counts.

\# Voice DNA

## Writing Rules
- Write like a sharp human, not a language model.
- Use contractions naturally (don't, can't, won't).
- Short paragraphs. 1-3 sentences max.
- Get to the point. No throat-clearing, no preamble.
- If making a claim, be specific. Use numbers, names, concrete details.
- Vary sentence length. Mix short punchy lines with longer ones.
- Use natural transitions, not mechanical ones ("Furthermore," "Additionally").
- When uncertain, say so plainly ("I think," "probably," "kinda"). Hedging is human.
- Never pad output to seem more thorough. Shorter and accurate beats longer and fluffy.
- Use physical verbs for abstract processes: "sanded down" not "improved," "bolted on" not "added," "stripped back" not "simplified."
- Humor comes from specificity, not from jokes. Be unexpectedly precise.
- Parenthetical asides are good. Use them for editorial commentary, honest reactions, quick tangents, and deflating your own seriousness (like this).

## Formatting Rules
- Short paragraphs (1-2 sentences default, 3 max).
- Numbers as digits.
- Contractions always.
- NO em dashes ever. Use commas, periods, colons, semicolons, or parentheses.
- Bold sparingly, 1-2 key moments per section.
- Code blocks for specific prompts, commands, or tool outputs.

## Banned Phrases (never use these, ever)

### Dead AI Language
- "In today's \[anything\]..."
- "It's important to note that..." / "It's worth noting..."
- "Delve" / "Dive into" / "Unpack"
- "Harness" / "Leverage" / "Utilize"
- "Landscape" / "Realm" / "Robust"
- "Game-changer" / "Cutting-edge"
- "Straightforward"
- "I'd be happy to help"
- "In order to"

### Dead Transitions
- "Furthermore" / "Additionally" / "Moreover"
- "Moving forward" / "At the end of the day"
- "To put this in perspective..."
- "What makes this particularly interesting is..."
- "The implications here are..."
- "In other words..."
- "It goes without saying..."

### Engagement Bait
- "Let that sink in" / "Read that again" / "Full stop"
- "This changes everything"
- "Are you paying attention?"
- "You're not ready for this"

### AI Cringe
- "Supercharge" / "Unlock" / "Future-proof"
- "10x your productivity"
- "The AI revolution"
- "In the age of AI"

### Generic Insider Claims
- "Here's the part nobody's talking about"
- "What nobody tells you"
- Anything with "nobody" or "most people don't realize"

### The Big One (FATAL)
- "This isn't X. This is Y." and ALL variations.
- "Not X. Y."
- "Forget X. This is Y."
- "Less X, more Y."
- ANY sentence that negates one framing then asserts a corrected one.
- If even ONE of these appears, the output fails. Delete the negation, just state the positive claim.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do consultants actually get clients from LinkedIn?

Not from going viral. The consultants who win clients on LinkedIn run a system: a profile built like a landing page, a consistent posting cadence (three to five times a week), and posts engineered around how the feed actually works. Engagement in the first 90 minutes decides reach, comments matter more than likes, and links belong in the first comment instead of the post. Do those consistently and LinkedIn becomes a steady source of inbound calls.

### How often should a consultant post on LinkedIn?

Three to five times a week, consistently, beats a burst of daily posts followed by a month of silence. Consistency is what the algorithm and your audience both reward. The hard part isn't the cadence, it's sustaining it on top of a full client load, which is where most consultants drop off around week five.

### Why do most consultants fail at LinkedIn?

Time, not ideas. Writing three or four sharp posts a week is effectively a part-time job, and it's the first thing dropped when a client deadline lands. A week off becomes a month, momentum resets, and the presence dies. The fix is to stop starting from a blank page and instead turn material you already have (calls, client work, your own thinking) into posts.

## The Playbook

Ten chapters. Click any one to open it.

01 Pick the voices, and the territory each one owns

3–5 voices to start, each owning a lane nobody else touches. Half a day to map it.

Not everyone should post. Pick the few whose voice already belongs in your market and who have something real to say to your buyers. The rest of the team has a job, but it comes later.

-   CEO. The vision and the hard calls.
-   Head of product. How the thing actually gets built.
-   Sharpest engineer. The technical truth no competitor will admit.
-   Head of sales. What buyers get wrong before they buy.

**Worth knowing.** Write each lane as one sentence: this person owns this topic for this audience. If two overlap, pull them apart until each person has a corner of the market that is theirs alone.

**The trap.** You hand everyone “thought leadership” and they all post the same vague takes. The feed sees four versions of one person, and overlap kills reach.

3 lanes

One 30-person startup gave the founder “why we exist,” the head of sales “what buyers get wrong,” and a senior engineer “how we really build it.” Zero overlap, three audiences that barely knew the other two were posting.

02 Build every profile as a landing page

60–90 min per person, once. Then small edits as the positioning sharpens.

A profile is the first thing a buyer checks after they see a post. Most read like a resume, and that leaks every bit of reach you fought to earn.

-   Headline. The only part shown before the cutoff. Say what you do and who for, not your job title.
-   About. Open with the problem you solve. Write it like a person talking, not a job application.
-   Featured. Pin the one post or result that proves you know your stuff.
-   Banner. Trade the stock skyline for a single line about who you help.
-   Photo. A real face, cropped close, looking at the camera.

**Worth knowing.** Under about 2,000 followers, send connection requests to your target buyers instead of chasing followers. Connections see your posts far more reliably.

**The trap.** Pouring months of posts into a profile that converts nobody. The reach lands, the visitor reads a job application, and they leave.

↑ a third

One founder changed only the headline and the pinned post. Profile visits to booked calls rose by a third in six weeks. Same posts, better front door.

03 Set a cadence the team can actually keep

2/week Two real posts beat seven rushed ones. Thirty minutes to set the rhythm, then hold it.

Decide how often each person posts, then hold the line. The feed rewards the finished posts and quietly forgets the rest.

-   Put it on a calendar. Assign days so two people never go quiet in the same week, and the team is not all loud on one morning.
-   Post when buyers are awake. For most B2B, Tuesday to Thursday, 8–10am or 12–2pm their time. A starting guess, not a rule.
-   Tune after a month. Check each person's analytics and move to their real best window.

**The trap.** You start at five posts a week each, burn out by week three, and go dark. The gap erases the gains, and the feed forgets the team fast.

3×/wk

One team dropped from daily to three times a week per person. Total reach went up, because every post was actually finished before it went out.

04 Capture raw material continuously, across the team

Minutes/day Spread across the team, it never stops. This is the input the whole system runs on.

The hard part is not writing. It is having something worth saying every week without repeating yourself. The good stuff happens live, in standups, sales calls, and Slack rants, then disappears by lunch.

-   Voice memo. Right after a sales call, in the car, while it is still fresh.
-   Screenshot. A sharp customer line, a surprising metric, a good rant.
-   One shared channel. Everyone drops fragments in. No format, no pressure, just raw fuel piling up.

**Worth knowing.** The objection you have now answered three times this week is already a post. The lines that feel too obvious to mention are usually the ones buyers want.

**The trap.** You sit down Monday to write, the well is dry, so you post something safe and forgettable. Generic posts train your audience to scroll past your name.

0 blank pages

One team keeps a channel called “post fuel.” Every objection, every surprising metric, every good rant goes there. Nobody on that team has started from a blank page in months.

05 Run a mix of post types, not one note

4 types Rotate them so no two posts in a row feel the same. An hour to plan, then one quick choice each time.

One kind of post trains your audience to expect one thing, then scroll past it. The feed rewards range. Teaching on Monday, a hard take on Wednesday, a story on Friday keeps you interesting for months.

-   Proof. Teach the thing you know cold.
-   Contrarian. Say what your market is too polite to say out loud.
-   Build in public. Show a real number or a real mistake.
-   Story. Let one moment from the week carry the point.

**The trap.** Finding a format that works and running it into the ground. The fifth “five lessons I learned” post pulls a fraction of the first. The feed has seen it, and so has your audience.

1 post/wk

One founder posted nothing but tactical how-tos for a month and flatlined. He added one honest “here is what we got wrong” post a week, and that single format pulled more inbound than the other four put together.

06 Write the hook last

210 chars All a phone shows before “see more.” The hook has to land inside it, in two lines.

The first line decides if anyone reads the rest. Write the whole post first, then hunt for the most surprising sentence in it, usually buried in the middle, cut it, and move it to the very top.

-   Contrarian reframe. “Everyone says X. Here is why that is wrong.”
-   Named proof. “This company did X without the obvious method.”
-   Plain observation. “Here is what most people miss about X.”

**Worth knowing.** Keep lines under a dozen words. Skip the warm-up openers: no “I have been thinking about,” no “Today I learned.” Start on the point.

**The trap.** Writing the hook first, falling in love with it, and burying the real insight in paragraph four where nobody reaches it.

Door vs wall

“We almost shut down in March” beats “Some thoughts on resilience.” One is a door. The other is a wall.

07 Engineer each post for the feed

10 min Of formatting per post. It decides how far the thumb scrolls before it moves on.

Format is not decoration. Short lines. One idea per line. A blank line after every sentence or two. Nothing longer than two lines in a row. Write for a phone, because that is where it gets read.

-   Links in the first comment. The feed buries posts that send people off-platform. Put the link below, not in the body.
-   Match format to idea. A single sharp thought is a text post. A step-by-step or teardown is a carousel, which the feed pushes once it earns comments.

**The trap.** Pasting a polished paragraph straight from a doc. It looks like effort and reads like a chore, and people bounce in half a second.

2× dwell

The same words, broken into single lines with space between them, can double the time people spend on a post. Same idea, different shape.

08 Repurpose one thought across every voice

1 idea, 4 posts The thinking is done once. The reshaping is fast. A small team posts like one ten times its size.

One good insight is not one post. It is the founder's take, a sharper version from the engineer, a line in the newsletter, and a comment thread from the head of sales. Each person says it from their own seat.

-   Founder. Gives the why.
-   Engineer. Gives the how.
-   Salesperson. Gives what the customer actually said.

**Worth knowing.** The rule that keeps it honest: same idea, different angle, never the same words. Three real posts, not one post pasted three times.

**The trap.** Everyone copies the same post and the feed sees duplicates, which it demotes on sight. Or worse, only one person ever speaks and the rest of the team's reach goes unused.

3 posts, 1 decision

A pricing change became the founder's “why we did it,” the head of sales' “what customers said,” and the engineer's “what we had to rebuild.” Three audiences, one decision.

09 Rally the team behind the posts that matter

90 min The feed sets a post's ceiling in the first ninety minutes, off who engages early and how.

Not every post deserves the whole team. The best ones do. When someone lands a post that matters, a sharp take, a launch, a story worth spreading, the rest of the team shows up behind it.

-   Comment. Add a second point, not “great post.”
-   Reshare. Push it to an audience the author does not reach.
