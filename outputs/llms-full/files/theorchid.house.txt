## Property Identity and Positioning

The Orchid House is an entire-place vacation rental in Orchid Tree Lane Estates within the Sunrise Park area of Palm Springs, California. The owner-published site presents the residence as built in 1971. It is positioned as a bright, color-rich home base for families and friends, with an emphasis on shared meals, pool days, mountain views, and movement between indoor and outdoor living.

The public brand line is "Palm Springs, in full color." The site describes a rhythm of slow mornings, sun-soaked afternoons, dinners beneath the palms, a soak beneath the stars, and enough room for a group to gather without everyone occupying the same space.

The house combines a central open kitchen, dining area, and living room with four distinct bedrooms, three bathrooms, a separate Game Room, a private pool courtyard, an outdoor dining and barbecue area, a side lawn, front courtyard, laundry room, and multiple lounge areas. The website compares the experience to having a small private resort.

## The House Experience

The main living areas are intentionally connected. Breakfast at the kitchen island can move into a meal at the dining table, lounging by the fireplace, a ping-pong match, or a walk out to the pool terrace without separating the group. The site repeatedly emphasizes that conversation can continue even while people spread between rooms.

The current visual presentation uses a mid-century Palm Springs vocabulary without treating the house as a museum. Warm white remains dominant, while flat fields of cobalt, sky blue, turquoise, citrus yellow, apricot, tangerine, and raspberry identify rooms and gathering zones. Walnut and dove-gray details keep the palette residential. There are no gradients or decorative shadows in the brand system; color, geometry, photography, and clean lines do the work.

## Interior Gathering Spaces

### Kitchen

The kitchen opens directly to the dining and living spaces. Current photography shows cobalt cabinetry, white counters, a broad island, island seating, a range, ovens, and generous preparation surfaces. The public site calls it fully equipped, and the selected guest reviews repeatedly describe it as unusually well stocked.

The living room has colorful mid-century seating, a sky-blue fireplace wall, a geometric tangerine, pink, and yellow feature wall, a fireplace, and a 65-inch smart television. Seating is arranged for conversation, movies, and relaxing within view of the connected living areas.

The separate Game Room contains an indoor ping-pong table and raspberry accent wall. Its separation from the main living room gives the house an additional activity zone without disconnecting it from the rest of the group experience.

## Detailed Architectural and Development History

### Evidence and Attribution Standard

The history of the broader development is better documented than the history of this individual house. Three claims must be kept separate:

1. The owner-published property website and its structured data identify The Orchid House as a residence built in 1971.
2. Municipal research, preservation records, and period advertising establish Howard P. Lapham as the designer-developer of Orchid Tree Lane Estates beginning in 1966.
3. No source reviewed for this document establishes that Lapham personally designed this individual 1971 residence.

Lapham should be described as a registered designer, architectural designer, designer-developer, contractor, draftsman, land planner, carpenter, or developer according to context. Preservation sources specifically distinguish his architectural-designer status from licensed architects whose work has traditionally received more attention.

The claims below are grounded in the linked city survey, preservation nomination, museum and Getty finding aids, obituary, and period material reproduced by those sources.

### What Is Documented About The Orchid House

The property website identifies the house as built in 1971 and places it within Orchid Tree Lane Estates. The current residence has an observable low profile, open central gathering rooms, direct circulation toward the pool courtyard, broad indoor-outdoor relationships, and a lifestyle organized around shade, water, shared meals, and outdoor living.

Those features can reasonably be discussed alongside themes documented in Lapham's regional modernism, but the comparison is interpretive. It is not proof that his office drew the plans for this specific house.

The reviewed public record does not establish:

- The original street address used in the 1971 permit record
- The first owner or commissioning client
- The architect or designer named on an individual building permit
- The builder of this specific residence
- The original elevation or plan type
- Whether it was custom, tract, or a later infill house
- Whether its plans survive in the Howard P. Lapham Collection
- Whether the 1971 date represents permit, completion, assessment, or another property record
- Its exact relationship to the 1966 Patio Home model at 314 Orchid Tree Lane
- A complete alteration, renovation, or restoration history

The City of Palm Springs survey itself leaves two central questions open: whether Lapham designed the individual residences and whether they were custom or tract homes. No reviewed permit, original plan, deed record, or archival drawing identifies The Orchid House as a documented individual Lapham commission.

The evidence-conscious formulation is therefore:

> The Orchid House is a 1971 residence within Orchid Tree Lane Estates, a development advertised in 1966 with Howard P. Lapham as designer-developer. The surviving evidence does not establish that Lapham personally designed this individual house.

### Orchid Tree Lane Estates Before the 1971 House

City preservation research traces Orchid Tree Lane Estates to 1966. Lapham subdivided a single street of residences on Orchid Tree Lane between Alejo Road and Amado Road. The development contained 22 parcels.

The advertised program included:

- Three- and four-bedroom plans
- Two- and three-bathroom plans
- Family rooms
- All-electric kitchens
- Air conditioning
- Marketing associated with Southern California Edison's promotion of electricity-driven living
- Homes described in advertising as "individually styled"

The Mid-Century Modern, post-and-beam model was called the Patio Home and stood at 314 Orchid Tree Lane.

A reproduced November 1966 Desert Sun advertisement adds important detail. It:

- Identifies Howard P. Lapham as "designer-developer"
- Announces the Group II grand opening
- Gives the dates as November 26 and 27
- Names 314 Orchid Tree Lane as the model-home address
- Advertises the homes as "custom built by" Coble & Wessman Construction Company, Inc.

That builder language is preserved as an attributed advertising claim. It does not prove that Coble & Wessman constructed this individual 1971 house.

The Desert Star nomination bibliography records period newspaper coverage connected with the development on April 15, June 25, November 18, November 25, November 26, and December 2, 1966. The April item concerned art for the model home; the later dates track launch and sales activity. These dates are cited by the nomination rather than independently reproduced in full here.

Publicly indexed research has not yet produced a complete tract map, subdivision brochure, permit ledger, or period street-scene archive comparable to the records available for some better-known Palm Springs tracts. The Palm Springs Art Museum archive, Palm Springs Historical Society, municipal permit records, and unindexed Desert Sun material are the strongest next places to investigate.

## Howard P. Lapham: Detailed Biography

Howard P. Lapham was born in Oklahoma City on May 11, 1914. Before coming to the desert he worked in or came from Stamford, Connecticut. He moved to the Coachella Valley in 1954 at age 40.

The biographical record says he initially intended to work as a builder. Within roughly a year, however, he was designing residences for wealthy members of Thunderbird Country Club along the fairways and in Thunderbird Heights. That quick shift placed him in the active postwar market that was transforming Palm Springs, Rancho Mirage, and Palm Desert.

Archival and preservation sources describe a practical, trade-based background. Depending on the source and period, Lapham is identified as:

- Registered designer
- Architectural designer
- Developer
- Carpenter
- Draftsman
- Contractor
- Land planner

That combination helps explain the breadth of his practice. He moved among custom houses, resort hotels, club facilities, shops, banks, restaurants, medical buildings, commercial centers, planning studies, land-development concepts, and residential tracts. He could participate in design, development, construction thinking, and site planning rather than occupying only one professional role.

Lapham married Rita Leeney in 1956. Rita died in 2001. Their sons were Lawrence and Robert; Lawrence predeceased Howard. Howard died in his sleep in Palm Springs on April 16, 2008, at age 93.

He formally retired in the 1980s but continued maintaining an office at 577 East Sunny Dunes Road in Palm Springs afterward. A later family remembrance says he kept regular office hours into advanced age, but that anecdote is secondhand. The firm documentary claim is that he maintained the office after formal retirement.

### The Missing 2002 Interview

The City of Rancho Mirage historic-resources survey cites an October 4, 2002 interview with Howard Lapham conducted by Peter Moruzzi. The Desert Star nomination relies on the resulting biographical summary and describes it as one of the few primary sources available on Lapham.

No public scan, transcript, audio recording, or detailed archive record for the original interview was located during this research. That gap matters because many later summaries appear to descend from the same oral-history chain. Locating Moruzzi's notes, recording, or correspondence would materially improve the documentary foundation for Lapham scholarship.

## Development of Lapham's Practice

### 1950s: Establishing a Desert Practice

Lapham arrived during a period of rapid resort and residential growth. His early record spans plumbing-supply and furnishing stores, hotels, residences, a small modern resort, and later a bus depot and nightclub remodel.

Desert Star, completed in 1956, became the most thoroughly analyzed example of his early modernism. Its small units were organized around a pool courtyard, and the property later became central to the preservation case for Lapham's importance.

By 1959 he was connected with two very different public-facing commissions: the Palm Springs Greyhound Bus Depot and the remodel of the Chi Chi Club. That range, from transit building to nightlife landmark, is characteristic of a practice that resisted a single building type.

### 1960s: Residences, Clubs, Commercial Work, and Development

The early 1960s brought residences for Thunderbird-area clients, club facilities, and Architectural Digest exposure. The Peter Kiewit, Hyatt Robert von Dehn, W. Clarke Swanson, and Bill Morrow residences, as well as Thunderbird Country Club work, place Lapham in the same high-end regional market served by better-known modernists.

His practice also expanded through collaboration and commercial work. Palm Springs National Bank, Smoke Tree Village, banks at Smoke Tree, office and warehouse projects, Lord Fletcher's Inn, and Orchid Tree Lane Estates show movement between individual design commissions and development-scale work.

The 1966 estate advertisement is especially revealing because it uses the combined title "designer-developer." That phrase captures the practical overlap in Lapham's career more accurately than a narrow label.

### 1970s and 1980s: Continued Regional Work and Planning

The later project record includes club work, bank and hotel recreation facilities, restaurants, professional offices, commercial centers, and planning concepts. The archival inventory extends into the 1980s and includes land-use and redevelopment studies. Some late entries may be concepts rather than completed buildings, but they demonstrate that his office continued to engage with the region at multiple scales.

The following chronology combines projects listed by municipal surveys, the Desert Star nomination, and the Palm Springs Art Museum finding aid. It is not a definitive catalogue raisonne. Some entries are securely built and documented; others are archival leads or projects whose precise date, authorship, or completion status remains unsettled.

### Additional Archival Leads

The museum finding aid contains many other project titles, including development master plans, convention and conference concepts, medical-science and office parks, resort and vacation villages, Smoke Tree studies, Ramon-area concepts, Pueblo Viejo or Old Town work, and aerial planning drawings.

A finding-aid entry is evidence that material exists in the collection, not automatic proof that Lapham authored a built project. His office files also contain reference or associated drawings explicitly credited to William F. Cody and other designers. The archive is therefore a research map, not a clean authored-work list.

## Collaborators and Professional Context

### Richard Harrison

Preservation records explicitly credit architect Richard Harrison with Lapham on:

- Palm Springs National Bank
- Smoke Tree Village
- The 1975 Bank of America project at Smoke Tree Village

Harrison should be described as a documented collaborator, not as a competitor in those projects.

### Haralamb H. Georgescu

The Palm Springs Art Museum finding aid says some Lapham buildings from 1959 through 1963 were designed with assistance from Romanian-born architect Haralamb H. Georgescu, also known in American records as Harlan Georgesco. The Getty Research Institute says Georgescu began working with Lapham on Palm Springs projects in 1959.

The available summaries do not provide a reliable project-by-project division of design authorship. The Getty finding aid catalogs Chi Chi Starlite Room material as 1956, while Lapham's Chi Chi remodel is normally dated to 1959; that entry therefore does not establish Georgescu's role in the later remodel. Georgescu's archive also includes Palm Springs material such as the Crosby House, but exact roles should be determined from the drawings rather than inferred from overlapping dates.

### Regional Peers

Lapham competed in the same market as [William F. Cody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Francis_Cody), [E. Stewart Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._Stewart_Williams), [Donald Wexler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Wexler), William Krisel, Hugh Kaptur, and other prominent desert modernists. Saying he worked "alongside" them can be misunderstood as collaboration. The stronger documented point is that he pursued comparable regional clients and commissions while holding a different professional status.

His residences and Thunderbird Country Club work appeared in Architectural Digest, demonstrating contemporary recognition even though later architectural histories often gave more attention to licensed architects.

## Documented Design Vocabulary

The Desert Star nomination supplies the clearest close reading of a Lapham building. It should not be treated as a universal formula for every Lapham project, but it documents the following features at Desert Star, many of which also reflect broader Desert Modern practice:

- Strong horizontal massing
- Intersecting shed and flat roof forms
- Deep eaves that moderate desert sun
- Clerestory windows
- Metal casement windows
- Large sliding-glass doors
- Courtyard and pool organization
- Axes oriented toward mountain views
- Expressed columns and beams
- Integrated geometric planters
- Wood paneling
- Broad areas of glass
- Stucco
- Concrete slumpstone
- Modest man-made materials
- Visual continuity between indoor and outdoor space

The Orchid House website interprets this residence's low profile, open rooms, and direct pool relationship as carrying the spirit of Lapham's Desert Modern work. That is a property interpretation, not an archival authorship finding.

## Recognition and Preservation

Lapham's status as an architectural designer rather than a licensed architect contributed to his omission from standard lists of Palm Springs architects. The 2016 Desert Star nomination directly addressed that imbalance. It described him as a respected and locally prominent modernist practitioner, argued that his body of work deserved fuller appreciation, and evaluated Desert Star as the work of a master under the city's designation criteria.

On October 5, 2016, the Palm Springs City Council approved Desert Star as a Class 1 Historic Site by a 4-0-1 vote. The Palm Springs Preservation Foundation prepared and advocated for the nomination; the City Council made the designation.

Desert Star is a preservation success, but other Lapham work has been vulnerable. [Preservation Mirage](https://www.preservationmirage.org/lost-architecture) documents the Hyatt Robert von Dehn residence as demolished in 2020. That loss illustrates the uneven survival of buildings by designers whose reputations are still being reconstructed.

Lapham's place in public architectural history remains less developed than that of several peers. Museum and preservation discourse increasingly includes him in the same Palm Springs constellation, but dedicated scholarship, public archives, and building-level research remain incomplete.

## The Howard P. Lapham Collection

In July 2024 the Palm Springs Historical Society donated the Howard P. Lapham Collection to the Palm Springs Art Museum. The finding aid describes:

- Material dated from 1946 through 2004
- Bulk dates from 1960 through 1970
- Architectural drawings arranged by project name
- Six flat-file drawers
- Access for qualified researchers by appointment
- Copyright and literary rights retained by creators and heirs

The collection substantially improves the research landscape because it identifies project material that is not well represented in public histories. It also requires careful use. Some folders contain reference or associated drawings credited to other architects, and some entries are planning studies, concepts, or projects with uncertain construction status.

The Palm Springs Historical Society, Palm Springs Art Museum, Getty Research Institute's Georgescu papers, city permit records, and unindexed newspaper collections are the most promising repositories for further work.

## Family and Regional Legacy

Howard and Rita Lapham had two sons, Lawrence and Robert. Lawrence "Larry" Lapham became an architect and developer in his own right and worked in partnership with Hugh Kaptur. The [Tahquitz Plaza nomination](https://www.pspreservationfoundation.org/pdf/TahquitzPlazaClass1NomPSPFFINAL.pdf#page=10) gives the partnership as 1967 through 1976 and says the firm operated as Kaptur-Lapham & Associates beginning in 1972.

That nomination documents Larry's role with Kaptur on Tahquitz Plaza and his participation in LBC Enterprises with Bill Bachwick and Philip E. Crystal, developers of Desert Dorado near Sunrise and Alejo. This is a nearby family and development connection, not evidence that Larry designed Orchid Tree Lane Estates or continued Howard's specific 1966 project.

## Unresolved Research Questions

The history remains incomplete in several important ways:

1. **Individual house authorship:** Who designed this specific 1971 residence?
2. **Permit and completion evidence:** What document supports the 1971 date?
3. **Builder:** Was this house constructed by Coble & Wessman, another contractor, or as later infill?
4. **Plan and elevation:** Which, if any, 1966 estate plan corresponds to the house?
5. **Custom versus tract:** How should the development's advertising language be reconciled with city-survey uncertainty?
6. **Original owner:** Who first commissioned or occupied the house?
7. **Alteration history:** Which features are original, restored, renovated, or newly introduced?
8. **Archive match:** Do drawings for this address or parcel survive in the Lapham Collection?
9. **2002 interview:** Where are Peter Moruzzi's original notes, audio, or transcript?
10. **Project catalogue:** Which museum finding-aid entries were built, which were concepts, and which are reference material by other designers?
11. **666 Building:** Can the conflicting date and Howard/Lawrence attribution be reconciled?
12. **Georgescu collaboration:** Which drawings establish each collaborator's role on specific projects?

These are not minor footnotes. They define the boundary between a well-supported development history and an unsupported claim of individual architectural authorship.
