## Focus Areas (Historical Experience)

### Sales
Founder-led sales, first 10-50 customers, sales playbooks, CRM pipeline, first sales hire

**Approach:**
1. Know your buyer better than they know themselves — in hard industries, your buyer's world is completely different from yours. Speaking their language matters more than any methodology.
2. Build the process while you sell — every deal you close as a founder is a chance to document what works. You're not just selling — you're building the playbook your first sales hire will follow.
3. Earn the right to a long sales cycle — complex B2B deals take months. The founders who win add value at every stage: business cases, stakeholder alignment, proof of concepts.

### Marketing
ICP validation, positioning, messaging, demand generation, content strategy

**Approach:**
1. Positioning first — everything else is noise without it. Who are you for, why are you different, and how do your best customers find and trust you.
2. Presence second — be findable and credible. In complex B2B, your prospect is researching you before they ever take a call. If they Google you and find nothing, you've lost before you started.
3. Pipeline third — only after positioning and presence are solid do tactics start working. Content, events, outbound — these amplify a message that already resonates. Most founders jump straight here. That's why it doesn't work.

### Customer Success
Onboarding, activation, retention, expansion revenue, NRR optimisation

**Approach:**
1. Activation before retention — solve the first 48 hours before worrying about churn metrics and health scores. If you can't describe the customer journey step by step, you don't have a CS problem — you have a "nobody designed the experience" problem.
2. Implementation is your secret product lab — every onboarding call, every support ticket is a window into what your product actually does in the real world. The founders who treat implementation as a chore miss this entirely.
3. Systems before headcount — don't hire a CSM until you've personally onboarded enough customers to know what the playbook should be. Document the workflow, build the handoff from sales, set up the tooling — then hire someone to run the system you've proven works.

### People & Hiring
First commercial hires, team structure, onboarding, coaching frameworks, performance management

**Approach:**
1. Prove the role before you fill it — do the job yourself first. Close the deals. Run the onboarding. Once you know what good looks like, you can write a job description that describes reality — not a wishlist.
2. Hire for the stage, not the dream — at $200K ARR you don't need a VP of Sales. You need a hungry, coachable person who can execute a playbook you've already proven. Match the hire to the company you are today.
3. Structure is a gift, not micromanagement — onboarding plans, KPIs, regular check-ins, performance frameworks — these aren't corporate overhead. They're how you set someone up to win.

## Philosophy & Approach

### Operating Principles

**1. First person in**
You can't delegate a problem you haven't solved. As a founder, you need to be the first salesperson, the first CS rep, the first person in the room. Hire after you've done it yourself and know what good looks like.

**2. Do things that don't scale**
For your first 10–30 customers, forget scalability. Manual outreach, personalised demos, white-glove onboarding. Learn what works first. Systematise second. Scale third.

**3. Speed is our weapon**
You can't out-resource incumbents. But you can outpace them. Speed in outreach, speed in demos, speed to value. Weeks and days, not months and quarters. That's how early-stage wins.

**4. Save the cheerleader, save the world**
Win a tight beachhead, then use them as your wedge into the bigger market. Don't try to serve everyone at once. Pick the niche that unlocks the world you actually want.

**5. One attempt at winning**
In concentrated markets, you don't get second chances. Every demo, every email, every onboarding matters. No spray and pray. Prep like it's the only shot — because in vertical SaaS, it usually is.

**6. Stupidly obvious + highly detailed**
Whether it's a value prop, a blog post, or a sales deck — make it dead simple AND deeply specific. Vague loses credibility. Specific builds trust. Say exactly what the problem is, what the impact is, and how you fix it.

### My OpenClaw Platform Engineer

URL: https://www.sachee.com.au/the-markdown/my-openclaw-platform-engineer
Date: 2026-04-04

Using Claude Code as a platform engineer for an 11-agent OpenClaw instance — production upgrades, cron reliability surgery, and fleet-wide health audits.

OpenClaw agents are very good at brute forcing their way through tasks and being hyper proactive. Give them a task, they'll smash their head against it, get stuck, and then somehow come back five minutes later with exactly what you wanted. It's like watching a dog figure out a door handle. Ugly, but it works.

This is great for the day-to-day stuff. Small tasks, medium tasks, general operational grunt work. Especially if you have harnessed them with the right skills. The agents handle it.

But for the big stuff. Major version upgrades, building new agents from scratch, wiring up complex skill chains, infrastructure that touches everything. The agents aren't the right tool. They don't hold enough context. They can't see the whole board. They do one thing well and then lose the plot when it cascades into three other things.

Maybe it was out of habit, maybe it was because I'm lazy, but I've been using Claude Code more than OpenClaw itself to manage and run my OpenClaw instance. Not instead of the agents. Alongside them. Claude Code became the thing I'd open when I needed to think across the whole system — read the config, understand the state of all eleven agents, trace why a cron job was silently failing, work out which of the 43 ways my setup could break was actually breaking it this time.

Without going too deep into it, my OpenClaw setup has five business units with GMs for each unit and a total of eleven agents. 91 cron jobs. 7 Telegram groups with 40+ topic bindings.

One of the agents is a platform engineer called Cyclawps. Cyclawps runs routine health monitoring and is well scaffolded for the rinse and repeat tasks for new agents, skills or discovery. But when I need to do deep work or new developments that need me to think across the whole system; I open Claude Code.

Over four months of doing this, the notes, errors, and workflows I'd accumulated turned into something worth packaging. So I did.

And the timing is interesting. As of today, Anthropic has essentially banned OpenClaw from Claude subscriptions. Your agents now need to run on pay-as-you-go or cheaper models. Which means the big, meaty platform tasks — upgrades, debugging cascades, fleet-wide audits — the stuff that actually needs Opus-level thinking? You can still do all of that through Claude Code. Cyclawps is a Claude Code skill. Your agents run on whatever model makes economic sense (GLM-5 and MiniMax M2.7 are genuinely strong for daily agent work), and you open /cyclawps when you need the heavy lifting.

**1. Production upgrades across breaking changes:** Needed to upgrade from v2026.3.24 to v2026.4.2 — three intermediate releases, with breaking changes in every single one. Typed /cyclawps, pasted the release link. It fetched the release notes, mapped every breaking change against the specific config (11 agents, 91 cron jobs, 7 Telegram groups with 40+ topic bindings), and came back with a risk matrix. Flagged three critical risks and one pre-existing config error. Then built a 17-task plan across 5 phases. Pre-flight was handled by four subagents running in parallel. The actual upgrade had exactly 30 seconds of downtime. In the QA phase, it spun up four fresh Opus-model agents with zero context from the build session to audit the work — they caught three critical gaps the build agents missed.

**2. Cron reliability surgery:** 46 out of 107 cron jobs were in an error state. The biggest chunk (25 jobs) shared a single root cause: certain skills were trying to call the message tool directly during execution, but isolated cron sessions don't have a Telegram channel registered. Cyclawps helped trace this by cross-referencing all 107 jobs against their execution logs and categorizing every failure by its actual root cause. Deleted 20 stale jobs, bumped 4 timeouts, and deployed a watchdog probe.

**3. Fleet-wide health audits:** When running 11 agents, things drift. Files end up in the wrong directory. Memory documents bloat past size limits. Agent knowledge goes stale. A bootstrap audit found a 117KB deep-research output accidentally in Cyclawps's workspace root, being loaded into every single session. Another agent had 224KB of research files in its root. The hippocampus audit found 5 agents with domain violations — platform data leaking into advisory files, CRM details duplicating across agents. None catastrophic; all compound over time.

### 2,311 Customers and No Website: What Happens When Your Dad Has Product-Market Fit

URL: https://www.sachee.com.au/the-markdown/2311-customers-no-website-dad-product-market-fit
Date: 2026-03-27

Two thousand three hundred and eleven. That's how many customers my dad had successfully processed, invoiced, and fulfilled through WhatsApp before there was a single webpage with his name on it.

To understand how wild that is, you have to understand the macroeconomic reality of Sri Lankan e-commerce. Online shopping, at least as we define it in Western markets with slick Shopify checkouts and seamless Stripe integrations, barely exists here. It is a profoundly low-trust, cash-first economy. If someone buys something online, they absolutely will not part with their money until they are physically holding the product in their hands. Everything runs on Cash on Delivery (COD). The "checkout" isn't a cart; it's a conversation.

My dad, Jayantha, is a mechanical engineer by trade. He spent years running an engineering firm in Saudi Arabia before retiring in 2015 and moving back to Sri Lanka. But engineers never actually retire; they just find progressively more complicated ways to avoid having a normal hobby.

In 2023, for reasons even he hadn't entirely articulated to me, he started running Facebook ads for laser pointers. It was a tiny spark that somehow combusted into JDN Online, an island-wide event lighting and entertainment equipment business.

Every single transaction followed the exact same, brute-force path: Sourced via a viral Facebook ad. Converted via a highly manual, personal WhatsApp chat. Shipped across the country and paid for in cash on the doorstep.

My dad is turning 70 this year, and the man had somehow cracked the code on running really tight Facebook ad campaigns, with content and copy designed on Canva.

His entire back-office operation was held together by Microsoft Excel. He was spending his nights doing manual data entry, exporting CSVs, and cross-referencing courier waybills. This man had a functioning business. He just didn't have a business behind it.

**Three things worth pulling apart:**

1. What does it actually take to build infrastructure for a business that already works? A business with 2,311 customers and zero systems is not a greenfield build. It is a high-stakes retrofit. You cannot disrupt the existing revenue flow to introduce "better" technology.

2. Why did the website come second, and what had to come first? The answer reveals a fundamental truth about how AI tooling actually functions in the real world. The very first thing built wasn't the website — it was an image generator. How do you photograph a laser? Google dropped nanobanana on the day, so standard smartphone photos were fed into the model to render professional hero shots.

3. How do you build for an elderly user, and a market, that inherently distrusts the internet? The site had to be trilingual from the first commit — English, Sinhala, and Tamil. This doubled the complexity of the routing and the CMS schema, but it was non-negotiable. Instead of 'add-to-cart' it's a 'WhatsApp' button — the website was never designed to replace the WhatsApp conversation. In a low-trust economy, that conversation is the closing mechanism.

The core website sprint took 72 hours (September 29th to October 2nd, 2025). Built with Next.js, TypeScript, Tailwind, Shadcn UI, and Sanity CMS. Session replays revealed visitors bouncing after struggling to change language — increased touch targets to 44px and added auto browser language detection.

The lesson: true scale isn't a marketing problem. Scale is what happens when you solve the boring, unsexy problems — the product catalogue, the inventory tracking, the trilingual database schema, the 44-pixel touch targets — well enough that the business stops being solely dependent on one founder's inbox.

### Hippocampus: Giving Bobo a Working Memory

URL: https://www.sachee.com.au/the-markdown/hippocampus-giving-bobo-a-working-memory
Date: 2026-03-15

Building a three-layer memory system for AI agents — daily notes, rolling synthesis, and permanent memory — then packaging it as an OpenClaw plugin.

Last July after leaving the business, started building a way to document and systematise everything — all the concepts, frameworks, thoughts, snippets of randomness about building and launching businesses. This manifested in a monolithic markdown files directory plugged into Claude Code, including a CRM.

The next logical step was a custom Next.js app. Created a web app with CRM data, files and thoughts living in Supabase. The app had a chatbot named Bobo with its own personality and self-learning — updating itself and all data every night via 2 cron jobs.

But every new session, Bobo would wake up confused about what's relevant. No memory of yesterday's conversations, pipeline deals, or commitments. Five minutes of every session spent re-explaining everything.

Eventually landed on modelling the memory around a literal brain. Three layers: 1) daily notes as raw memory generated from chats and file updates, 2) a rolling synthesis as working memory, and 3) permanent memory for things that stuck around long enough to matter. Like the early memory implementation from Claude.

When this was implemented, conversations with Bobo became more useful. Bobo started waking up knowing what was going on.

When OpenClaw dropped, started migrating everything over — all the files, contexts, patterns, and workflows.

Hippocampus is that pattern turned into a plugin. One install, your agents get a daily-synthesised briefing injected into every session — what's top of mind, what's open, what's owed. Not a log dump. A briefing.

Install: `openclaw plugins install @sacheeperera/hippocampus`

### The Yeah-Nahs of Product-Market Fit

URL: https://www.sachee.com.au/the-markdown/the-yeah-nahs-of-product-market-fit
Date: 2025-11-24

You can't sell your way to product-market fit. Find the overlooked segment that won't be polite. They're the ones who'll save you.

"Nah, your product looks great, mate. It just wouldn't work for us."

Three Swanny D's deep at an Australian Drilling Industry Association (ADIA) sundowner, talking to another drilling contractor MD. This is the fifth time hearing some version of this.

"Why not?" "We're different. Your product is designed more for the miners."

And that's when it made sense. They'd been overlooked for so long, they couldn't even imagine being THE customer. Every software vendor had built for the big mining companies. Contractors were expected to adapt, workaround, make it fit. So when someone actually built FOR them? Their first instinct was: "This can't be for us."

The Choice Every Early-Stage Founder Has to Make: Option A: Go out and try to sell. Option B: Go out and find people who'll help you build something worth selling. Most founders instinctively chase Option A. Except you're not validating the market — you're trying to systematize something that doesn't work yet. You're building a sales engine for a car that doesn't run. Because you can't sell your way to product-market fit. You can only learn your way there.

What Five Years at the Pub Taught Me: Spent the better part of five years talking to drilling contractors. Mostly at the pub. Didn't win customers — made friends. Real friendships across an entire industry. When a drilling contractor says "Yeah, nah you're wrong" — they're going to give you all the reasons why it won't work. When they say "Nah, Yeah but here's seven other things you're wrong about" — they're telling you exactly what you need to fix. That's not rudeness. That's a gift.

Save the Cheerleader, Save the World: Classic beachhead strategy. Win one tight segment so completely that it becomes your launchpad. Overlooked segments make ideal beachheads because: They have acute pain. They have no alternatives. They have real urgency. They have no patience for bullshit.

The Counterintuitive Truth About Product-Market Fit: True product-market fit means you can win the next 10 customers using the same core offering, positioning, and sales motion you used for the last 10. If every deal requires significant customization, you have customer fit but not market fit.

### The Stupidly Simple Strategy Framework

URL: https://www.sachee.com.au/the-markdown/how-to-write-stupidly-simple-strategy
Date: 2025-11-28

Most founders confuse complexity with strategy. If you can't explain it in 5 minutes, you don't have a strategy — you have diddly-squat.

The 3-Question Test: 1. What are we doing? 2. What are the goals? 3. How do we get there? If everyone can answer all three clearly and consistently, you have a strategy.

Strategy is simply a boring plan and your case for why you'll win using this plan.

What bad strategy looks like: The "Dog's Breakfast" (wish lists), The "Vomit" (50-slide decks), The "Buzzword Salad" (meaningless jargon), The "Layer Cake" (trying to be everything), The "Copy-Paste" (copying others without context).

The 4-Part Framework:

Phase 1: Diagnosis
- Part 1: The Situation — What is the market reality your customer lives in?
- Part 2: The Problem — Why is the current way unsustainable?

Phase 2: Strategy
- Part 3: The Difference (the zag) — "The market thinks the answer is X, but we know the answer is actually Y." A real difference requires a trade-off.
- Part 4: The Path Forward — The chronological sequence of moves.

Worked examples: Mining Software (CorePlan) — Served contractors first instead of miners, unlocking data for everyone. Hospitality Staffing (Sidekicker WA) — Pitched "Blended Workforce Technology" instead of "better temp agency" because Perth venues hated agencies. Sachee's Own Advisory — Specialization is fatal at early stage; founders need integrated architecture, not siloed specialists.

Why this works for early-stage ($0-1M): Resource Scarcity forces you to choose one problem. No Brand means your only leverage is a superior insight. Focus requires a ruthless sequence, not a wish list.

### The Cost of Building

URL: https://www.sachee.com.au/the-markdown/the-cost-of-building-and-why-i-m-still-figuring-out-how-to-talk-about-it
Date: 2025-11-16

### The High Cost of Low Tech: The Impact of Manual Operations on Drill Programs

URL: https://www.sachee.com.au/the-markdown/the-high-cost-of-low-tech-the-impact-of-manual-operations-on-drill-programs
Date: 2025-11-18

Spreadsheets and paper logs are killing efficiency. Here's what the exploration industry is leaving on the table by staying low-tech.

Many exploration companies still use paper and spreadsheets to manage drill programs. Most of the software available was built by traditional mining software companies focused only on the Majors and Enterprise. Mid tiers and juniors couldn't afford it, so they had no choice but to build their own or run manual workflows.

Key drill program metrics: Rig Utilisation, Cost per Meter, Penetration Rates, Core Loss, Breakdown Time, Sample Dispatch Turnaround, Consumables Cost, Fuel Usage.

Problems with traditional methods: Data entry errors, incorrect cost tracking, lost or misplaced data, delayed reporting, inconsistent data. According to McKinsey, mining productivity peaked in the 1990s and has declined by more than 30% since then.

Impact on drill programs: Reduced rig utilisation, increased cost per meter, slower penetration rates, increased standby time, core loss, hole deviation. Inaccurate data propagates through the entire database, leading to inaccurate resource estimates, mine planning, and lower profitability.

Benefits of digitising: EY found digital solutions can lead to cost savings of up to 20%. BCG research showed significant KPI improvements across companies that adopted technology. Improved accuracy, enhanced data analysis, streamlined collaboration.

### The Collaboration Revolution: How Technology is Redefining Work in Mining

URL: https://www.sachee.com.au/the-markdown/the-collaboration-revolution-how-technology-is-redefining-work-in-mining
Date: 2025-11-18

Mining technology is having its internet moment. Here's how collaboration tools are changing everything.

The shift from Web 1.0 to Web 2.0 revolutionized how people work. When the pandemic hit in March 2020, tools like Microsoft Teams and Zoom went from extras to essentials. According to IDC research, in just six months, adoption of collaboration tools grew as much as it had in the previous five years.

In mining, people used to rely on Excel, OneDrive, SharePoint, or on-prem systems. But a new generation of tools is changing everything.

The 5 Layers of Collaboration:
1. Communications Layer — real-time conversations, meetings, and messaging. In drill programs: status updates, approvals, field activity updates done seamlessly instead of on paper.
2. Content Layer — files, documents, data, and media. Integrating drill program operations platforms with databases for seamless data movement.
3. Productivity Layer — project management, automation, and integration. Tools like ClickUp, CorePlan, MX Deposits working together to remove manual tasks.
4. Insights Layer — real-time metrics, performance tracking, and analytics. Monitoring drilling efficiency, drill rates, and costs per meter in real-time.
5. Community Layer — breaking down silos, fostering collaboration across teams. Communities of geologists, drilling contractors, and management working toward common objectives.

The transformation is a process driven by culture. Putting the tech stack together is the easy part. The hard part is overcoming the belief that "the old way" was better.

### The Rise of the Digital Drill Contractor in Mineral Exploration

URL: https://www.sachee.com.au/the-markdown/the-rise-of-the-digital-drill-contractor-in-mineral-exploration
Date: 2025-11-18

Paper plods are becoming fossils. Choosing the right drill contractor now means picking one with digital-forward thinking.

Why drill contractors are going digital: Technological advancements making digital systems accessible and affordable. Increased competition requiring differentiation. Demand for real-time data and analytics. Environmental and social responsibility. Regulatory compliance.

Three types of digital systems: 1) Existing commercial solutions — established but may not cover specific needs. 2) Custom digital systems — tailor-made but resource-intensive to develop and maintain. 3) Modern drilling SaaS platforms like CorePlan or Krux — comprehensive, cloud-based solutions with built-in data validation, real-time data sharing, and analytics.

What to expect from a digital drill contractor: Efficient delivery of data through Excel formats. Faster data delivery compared to paper or manual systems. Improved data accuracy and validation. More time for geologists to focus on interpretation and planning. Enhanced data quality for resource modelling and estimation.

Benefits of modern SaaS platforms vs general/custom systems: Integrated governance and validation controls. Comprehensive supervisor review for precise data sharing. Secure data delivery through portals. Streamlined review, approval, and resubmission processes. Real-time access to daily drilling production and costs.

### Drilling into the Future: Is Your Drilling Data Ready for AI?

URL: https://www.sachee.com.au/the-markdown/drilling-into-the-future-is-your-drilling-data-ready-for-ai
Date: 2025-11-18

ChatGPT, Midjourney, Bard, and countless other AI tools are reshaping industries. A pressing question arises for mineral exploration: is drilling data ready for AI?

Traditional data management systems lack transparency and insight, with siloed franken-stacks of dated on-prem systems, SQL databases, and non-automated processes making it difficult to shift to data-driven operations.

Potential of AI in drilling data analysis: 1) Predictive analysis for strategic planning — AI predicting ground conditions and resource allocation. 2) Real-time insights for drill hole progress — AI-driven feedback on depth, angle, and geological formations. 3) Optimized data management and program budgeting — automated data categorization and cost prediction. 4) Safety and operational protocols — predicting potential hazards based on historical data. 5) Enhanced collaboration through data integration. 6) Post-drilling analysis and reporting.

Steps to make your company AI-ready: 1) Digitization — enable digital data capture with platforms like CorePlan or MXDeposits. 2) Digitalisation — streamline and automate internal processes. 3) Data-driven problem solving operations — upskill teams to use data effectively. 4) AI-driven operations — build trained models based on data-driven insights.

Key lesson from onboarding 80+ companies at CorePlan: start small and scale up. Organizations that aim for perfection from the start end up moving slower and stagnating.
