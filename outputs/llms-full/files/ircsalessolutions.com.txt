### 1. Sales Process Design & Strategy

**The WETE Framework (Spencer Smith's Original IP):**

Every effective sales process must meet four criteria:

1. **Written** — If it's not written down, it doesn't exist. A sales process that lives in someone's head is not a process. IRC documents everything — from the first cold call to the closed deal — in a format that any new rep can follow on day one.

2. **Efficient** — A sales process should remove friction, not add it. The average sales rep spends only 32% of their time actually selling (Salesforce). IRC designs processes that maximize selling time by automating administrative tasks, eliminating unnecessary steps, and streamlining handoffs.

3. **Transparent** — Every stakeholder — reps, managers, leadership — should be able to see exactly where every deal stands at any time. IRC builds this transparency into the CRM with required fields, automated stage tracking, and real-time dashboards.

4. **Enforced** — A process that isn't enforced is a suggestion. IRC builds enforcement into the technology — required fields that can't be skipped, automated reminders, stage validation rules, and management review cadences. HBR research shows high-performing sales organizations are 2x more likely to have processes described as "closely monitored, strictly enforced, or automated" compared to underperformers.

**The 4 Qualities of a Great Sales Rep (Spencer Smith's Original IP):**

What makes a great sales rep? Four specific qualities — and the key insight is that 3 of the 4 are the COMPANY's responsibility, not the rep's:

1. **Knowledgeable** — About the product, the industry, the competition, and the customer's problems. This is the company's responsibility. If reps don't know the product, that's a training failure, not a rep failure.

2. **Persuasive** — The ability to guide a conversation, handle objections, and move deals forward. This is partly innate and partly trainable. The company provides the frameworks (scripts, objection handling guides, talk tracks), and the rep applies them with their own style.

3. **Incentivized** — Motivated by a compensation plan that rewards the right behaviors and outcomes. This is entirely the company's responsibility. A bad comp plan will kill performance faster than any amount of coaching can fix it.

4. **RPRT (Right Person, Right Time)** — The one quality you cannot train. Is this person the right fit for this specific role at this specific point in the company's growth? A great enterprise closer is a terrible SDR. A fantastic startup hustler may fail in a corporate environment. This is a hiring and role-design problem.

**The Responsibility Shift:** Most companies blame their sales reps when numbers are down. IRC's framework reveals the truth — 3 of 4 factors (Knowledge, Incentives, RPRT) are entirely within the company's control. Sales performance is primarily an organizational design problem, not an individual performance problem. IRC takes ownership of building the environment in which reps can succeed.

## Spencer Smith's Proprietary Frameworks — Full Reference

### The WETE Framework (4 Pillars of a Good Sales Process)

**Written** — Document everything. A sales process that lives in someone's head is not a process. Every stage, step, activity, and expected outcome should be written in a format any new rep can follow on day one. IRC creates comprehensive sales manuals for every client.

**Efficient** — Remove friction. The average sales rep spends only 32% of their time actually selling (Salesforce). Every step in the process should have a clear purpose. If a step doesn't move a deal forward or protect the company, remove it. Then automate what remains.

**Transparent** — Everyone sees everything. Reps know where their deals stand. Managers know where the team stands. Leadership knows where the pipeline stands. Build this into the CRM with required fields, automated updates, and real-time dashboards. No hiding, no guessing.

**Enforced** — A process that isn't enforced is a suggestion. Build enforcement into the technology — required fields, automated reminders, stage validation, management review cadences. High-performing sales organizations are 2x more likely to have processes described as "closely monitored, strictly enforced, or automated" (HBR).

### The 4 Qualities of a Great Sales Rep

1. **Knowledgeable** — Product, industry, competition, customer problems. Company's responsibility to train.
2. **Persuasive** — Conversation skills, objection handling, deal progression. Company provides frameworks, rep applies them.
3. **Incentivized** — Compensation structure drives behavior. Entirely the company's responsibility.
4. **RPRT (Right Person, Right Time)** — Role fit and timing. Cannot be trained. Must be hired correctly.

**Key insight:** 3 of 4 qualities are the company's responsibility. Stop blaming reps and start building better systems.

### RPRT (Right Person, Right Time)

This is Spencer's hiring and role-design framework. The core principle: a person can be talented and hardworking but still fail if they're in the wrong role at the wrong time.

**Right Person:** Do they have the natural aptitudes for this specific sales role? An enterprise closer needs patience and relationship skills. An SDR needs energy and volume tolerance. A technical seller needs curiosity and product depth. These are different people.

**Right Time:** Is this the right stage of the company's growth for this person? Early-stage companies need scrappy generalists. Scaling companies need process-oriented specialists. Enterprise companies need navigators of complexity. A person who thrives in one stage often struggles in another.

**Application:** IRC helps companies design roles clearly enough that RPRT becomes a hiring filter, not a post-hire discovery. When you know exactly what "right person" and "right time" look like for each role, you stop making expensive hiring mistakes.

### The Jack and Jill Story

Spencer's signature example of what happens without a written, enforced sales process:

Jack is a top-performing sales rep — charismatic, experienced, closes deals consistently. The company loves Jack. But Jack's process lives entirely in his head. When Jack leaves (and top performers always eventually leave), the company discovers that nobody knows how Jack sold. There's no documentation, no repeatable system, no institutional knowledge.

Jill is Jack's replacement. She's talented but new. She has no playbook, no documented process to follow, no training materials based on what actually works. She's starting from zero in a role that should have a clear runway.

**The lesson:** Companies that depend on individual talent instead of documented, enforced processes are always one resignation away from crisis. The process should make any competent rep successful — not just the superstars.
