> Quilts by Big Wes (bigwes.com) is a custom quilt studio founded in 2013 by Wesley Hall, a professional quilter with over a decade of experience. The business creates handmade t-shirt quilts, memory quilts, memory bears, memory pillows, and offers longarm quilting services. All work is done by Wesley personally at CreateATL in Southwest Atlanta, GA. Prices start at $275. The company's mission is to honor memory through craft, making keepsakes that earn their place in a family.

## About the Company
**URL:** https://www.bigwes.com/about/

About the Company
Custom Quilts That Earn Their Place in a Family
Since 2013, Quilts by Big Wes has been turning clothing that matters into quilts that carry that meaning forward.

Our Story
A Practice Built Around Meaning
Quilts by Big Wes started in 2013 with a simple idea. The clothes people care most about rarely end up where they belong. Varsity jerseys get boxed up. A parent's favorite shirts end up in drawers nobody opens. Concert tees, race bibs, and dress uniforms collect in closets because nobody can bring themselves to let them go, and nobody knows what else to do with them.

A quilt solves that. A well-made one takes the garments out of storage and puts them somewhere the family actually lives. On a bed. Across the back of a couch. Wrapped around a grandchild. The story stays close instead of slipping further away.

More than a decade in, Quilts by Big Wes has made hundreds of custom quilts for families across Atlanta and beyond. Each one starts the same way: a free consultation, a careful look at the materials, and a conversation about what the finished quilt needs to feel like. The craft has grown since 2013. The commitment hasn't changed.

What Guides the Work
Mission and Vision

Mission: Honor memory through craft.
Every project Quilts by Big Wes takes on is built from something that already matters to the family bringing it in. The work is to protect that meaning, not compete with it. That means honest materials, honest timelines, and work a client can hand down without apology.

Vision: Keepsakes that earn their place.
A Quilts by Big Wes quilt is made to be used, loved, and handed forward. Not folded in a closet. Not kept for special occasions. The goal is a keepsake so well-built and so personal that it becomes part of the family the same way the garments inside it already are.

Why This Work Matters
A Quilt Can Hold What Other Gifts Can't
Most custom gifts are about the maker. Quilts by Big Wes is built around the opposite. The materials come from the client: their child's jerseys, their mother's blouses, their own wedding lace, their late father's work shirts. The quilt is made from the same fabric the memory lives in.

Clients tell us what the finished quilts do in the years that follow. A graduate takes one to college. A widow sleeps under one. A grandchild claims one that started as a grandparent's closet. These are the outcomes the work is built for. The quilt becomes a place where the memory stays within reach.

That is the bar every project is held to. Not "did we finish on time," though the work does. Not "did it look good in photos," though it does. The bar is whether the finished quilt deserves the garments that went into it. That standard is what the practice was founded on and what it still measures itself against.

What Is Longarm Quilting?
The Finishing Step Your Quilt Top Has Been Waiting For.
You designed the quilt. You chose the fabrics. You pieced the
blocks and pressed every seam. What comes next, getting that
top, batting, and backing stitched together into a finished quilt, is where longarm quilting comes in.
A computerized longarm machine moves across the quilt in precise,
repeating paths guided by a pantograph pattern. The result is
evenly distributed, professional-quality quilting from edge to
edge. It is the same technique used by production quilters,
done one quilt at a time in a working Atlanta studio.
You bring the top, I handle the rest. Drop it off at
CreateATL or ship it directly. When it is finished, you pick
up a completed quilt.
Schedule a Consultation

What Is Quilt Binding?
The Step That Finishes the Job
Binding is the fabric strip that wraps around the perimeter of a
completed quilt, enclosing the raw edges of the top, batting, and
backing. It is the final step before a quilt is done, and it has
a bigger impact on the finished look than most people expect.
Machine binding: faster, highly durable, visible machine stitching
on the quilt back. Ideal for everyday use quilts.
Hand binding: no visible machine stitching on the quilt back, softer
and more traditional edge. Popular for memory quilts and display quilts.
Binding can be ordered as a standalone service or combined with
longarm quilting in the same order.
Quilt Binding FAQ:
What is quilt binding? The fabric strip that finishes the raw edges of a quilt, enclosing top, batting, and backing.
Machine vs hand binding? Machine is faster and more durable. Hand has no visible stitching on the back.
Can I supply my own fabric? Yes. Or I can source a coordinating fabric.
Does my quilt need to be longarm quilted first? Yes, the layers must be quilted together before binding can be applied.
How is it priced? 30 cents per linear inch of perimeter. Quote confirmed at consultation.

What do you make?

About Our Services

What is the difference between Gallery Style and Collage Style?
Gallery Style uses uniform block sizes for a clean, symmetrical grid. Collage Style uses varied block sizes that match the scale of each graphic for a more dynamic layout. Both styles are available for all quilt sizes at the same price.

What is a memory bear?
A memory bear is a stuffed bear handmade from a meaningful garment, often clothing from a loved one who has passed. They are popular as standalone keepsakes or as companion pieces to a memory quilt. Memory bears start at $40 each.

What clothing types work for memory quilts?
Most garments: dress shirts, blouses, t-shirts, athletic wear, uniforms, baby clothes, and sentimental fabrics. Damaged or stained items can often be incorporated.

Can I use clothing from a loved one who has passed?
Yes. Memorial quilts are among the most meaningful projects Wesley takes on. He handles these with care, at the client's pace.

Can I include photos in my quilt?
Yes. Photo panels can be printed on fabric and incorporated. Non-fabric keepsakes like buttons or patches can sometimes be added as embellishments.

How many shirts do I need for a t-shirt quilt?
A Throw quilt typically uses 12-16 garments. A Queen typically uses 25-30. Garment counts vary by graphic size and layout style. Bring everything and Wesley will determine the best fit at the consultation.

Can I use shirts of different sizes?
Yes. Youth, XL, oversized, and all sizes in between are compatible.

Can I use a shirt that has a design only on the back?
Yes. Both the front and the back can be used as separate blocks.

Pricing & Timeline

What is the typical turnaround time?

How much does a custom quilt cost?

What sizes do you make?

Is a deposit required to start a project?
Yes. A deposit is required before work begins. The remaining balance is due when the finished quilt is ready for pickup or shipment. Your consultant will confirm the deposit amount during your free consultation.

Do you offer rush orders?
Rush availability depends on current workload. Please reach out directly at wesley@bigwes.com or 678-322-8849 and I will let you know what is possible for your timeline.

Shipping & Drop-Off

Do you ship finished quilts?
Yes. Finished quilts ship anywhere in the United States via USPS or UPS with insurance and tracking.

Can I drop off my materials in person?
Yes. Drop-off is by appointment at CreateATL, 900 Murphy Ave SW, Atlanta, GA 30310. If you are not local, you can ship your garments directly.

Materials & Craftsmanship

What backing and batting materials do you use?
I use quality cotton batting and a variety of backing fabrics. You can choose a solid or patterned backing from available options, or we can discuss custom backing fabric. All materials are selected for durability and comfort.

Can I add borders or custom binding to my quilt?
Yes. Borders and binding options are discussed during your consultation. Binding can be machine-sewn or hand-finished. If you have a specific fabric you want used for binding or borders, bring it to the consultation.

The Quilter Behind Quilts by Big Wes
I've been quilting professionally for over ten years, and I started Quilts by Big Wes with one straightforward goal: make quilts that people are genuinely proud to own. Not generic. Not rushed. Quilts built around the real stories and garments of the people who commission them.

I work out of CreateATL in Southwest Atlanta, a creative maker space that lets me run a fully equipped studio without the overhead of a standalone shop. Every quilt I make is sewn here, by hand, by me. There is no outsourcing, no factory production, and no shortcuts.

My specialty is custom t-shirt quilts and memory quilts, the kinds of projects where the client brings me something irreplaceable and trusts me to make it into something lasting. I take that seriously. I've never had a client regret the finished quilt.

The Craft
Quilting as a Discipline
I approach quilting the way any serious maker approaches their craft: with intention, attention to detail, and a genuine interest in getting better. I study the history of the form, particularly the traditions of African American quilting, and I bring that perspective to everything I make.

That background in quilt history, the study of Gee's Bend, Harriet Powers, the Underground Railroad canon, and the broader African American quilting tradition, informs how I think about fabric, pattern, and purpose. Even in a custom t-shirt quilt, those influences are present in how the work gets made.

When you hand me a box of your kid's game-worn jerseys or your mother's favorite blouses, I'm thinking about more than just layout. I'm thinking about what those things mean and what the quilt needs to feel like when it's done.

Speaking & Education

That conversation naturally draws on African American quilting traditions too, including the Underground Railroad quilt legend, Gee's Bend, Harriet Powers, and contemporary Black quilters. The goal is the same whether I'm at a guild meeting or a university lecture hall: put the craft and its stories in front of people who love the form.

## Blankets Are Not Quilts
**URL:** https://www.bigwes.com/blankets-are-not-quilts/

Atlanta, GA
Blankets Are Not Quilts
If you already bought a t-shirt blanket from a mail-in company and it did
not feel like the quilt you pictured, there is a reason. It was not built
like one. This page explains the difference and shows real Atlanta
clients who had it rebuilt.
Schedule a Free Quilt Consultation
The Core Difference
A Quilt and a Blanket Are Built Differently
A Custom Quilt: three layers (a pieced top, cotton batting, and a backing
fabric), stitched across the entire surface so the batting cannot shift
or bunch, each shirt cut and placed around its own graphic, built around
the client's layout, backing choice, and input at every step.
A Mail-In T-Shirt Blanket: two layers (a sewn panel of shirt squares and a
fleece backing), no batting or interfacing, joined only at the seams with
no all-over quilting, shirts cut into standardized squares by machine
which can crop a larger graphic, customization limited to size plus an
optional embroidery add-on in one corner.
These construction details for Project Repat are sourced from the
company's own help center ("does not include batting or interfacing")
and product pages ("perfect squares" cut with a clicker press), not from
a competitor's claim about them.
Real Clients, Real Redos
Three People Who Paid Twice, So You Do Not Have To
Case 1, T-Shirt Quilt: a client with four years of college shirts and two
years of first-job shirts had already ordered a Project Repat blanket.
It arrived with no batting, several graphics cropped to a standard
square, and no way to specify which shirts were featured. Quilts by Big
Wes rebuilt it as a Gallery-style custom quilt with cotton batting and
an all-over stipple quilting pattern.
Case 2, Memory Quilt, Graduation Gift: a mother ordered a mail-in t-shirt
blanket as a high school graduation gift from her daughter's four years
of band, soccer, and club shirts. It arrived without batting or
quilting stitches and with no input on layout or color. Quilts by Big
Wes rebuilt it in six weeks with a full consultation, cotton batting,
and the daughter's name and graduation year quilted into a corner block.
Case 3, Memory Quilt, Travel Shirts: a client with roughly a decade of
travel t-shirts (national parks, race shirts, cities visited) received a
mail-in blanket with several graphics cropped and no batting. Quilts by
Big Wes rebuilt it as a custom quilt with every graphic fully visible
and centered.
In two of these three cases, the client told Wesley directly that she
regretted paying for both products.
Side by Side
Custom Quilt vs. Mail-In T-Shirt Blanket
Construction: three layers (top, batting, backing) vs. two layers (panel,
fleece backing, no batting or interfacing).
Quilting stitches: stitched across the entire surface vs. no all-over
quilting, joined only at the seams.
Graphic placement: cut and arranged around each shirt's own graphic vs.
cut into standardized squares with a clicker press, which can crop a
larger graphic.
Design input: full consultation on layout, backing fabric, and quilting
pattern vs. limited to size selection plus an optional embroidery
add-on in one corner.
Where it is made: handmade at CreateATL in Atlanta, GA by Wesley Hall vs.
machine-assembled in Morganton, NC.
Typical turnaround: four to eight weeks vs. five to six weeks, per the
company's own site.
What "Custom" Means Here
You Are Part of Every Decision
A mail-in blanket is a configurator. A custom quilt is a conversation.
Clients choose the layout, the backing fabric, the quilting pattern, and
which shirt gets featured most prominently.
Questions
Blankets vs. Quilts: Frequently Asked Questions
Is a t-shirt blanket the same as a t-shirt quilt? No. A t-shirt quilt has
three layers held together by all-over quilting stitches. A t-shirt
blanket has two layers with no batting and no all-over stitching.
Can a t-shirt blanket be turned into a real quilt? Often, yes, if the
client kept the original shirts or has others at home.
How is a quilt actually constructed? Three layers, top, batting, and
backing, stitched through together across the entire surface.
How long does a custom quilt take? Four to eight weeks depending on size,
complexity, and current workload.
I already bought a t-shirt blanket from another company, can Wesley still

mail-in blanket did not turn out as pictured.

## Blog: Memory Bears Made from Clothing
**URL:** https://www.bigwes.com/quilt-blog/memory-bears-made-from-clothing/

Quilting Services · 2026-07-28 · By Wesley Hall

People come to me with a bag of clothing and a lot of feeling they do
not quite know what to do with. A parent's old work shirts. A
grandmother's housecoat. A baby's first outfit that fit for about six
weeks before it was outgrown. They are not ready to get rid of these
things, but a drawer or a box in the closet does not feel like enough
either. That is where a memory bear comes in.
A memory bear is a stuffed teddy bear handmade from garments that
actually belonged to someone. Not fabric that looks like the clothing.
The clothing itself. Cut, sewn, and stuffed into something you can hold
onto, put on a shelf, or pass down to someone who never got to meet the
person it came from. I make them here in Atlanta, and they start at $40
each.

What Kinds of Clothing Work for a Memory Bear?

The short answer is: most of it. If a garment was worn with meaning,
there is a very good chance I can turn it into a bear. The fabric just
needs to be sturdy enough to hold its shape once it is cut and sewn.
Soft, woven materials work especially well for the exterior of the bear.
T-shirts and casual shirts are the most popular by far. A well-worn
t-shirt carries something that a brand new piece of fabric never could.
Dress shirts and button-downs, a parent's everyday wardrobe, the shirts
they wore to work for thirty years, translate beautifully into a bear
with a lot of visual interest. Hoodies and sweatshirts, the worn-in
soft pieces that meant comfort, still do that job in bear form. Baby
and children's clothing, first outfits, tiny onesies, little-kid
favorites that were grown out of too fast, make some of the most
meaningful bears I produce. Uniforms, military, first responder, work,
and sports uniforms, represent service, sacrifice, or years of
dedication. Robes and loungewear carry quiet everyday memories that are
sometimes the most personal of all.
If you are not sure whether a specific item will work, reach out before
your consultation and we will talk it through. Wear, fading, and minor
staining are usually not a problem. That kind of history is part of
what makes the bear meaningful.

How Much Clothing Do You Need?

Typically one to two garments is enough for a single bear, depending on
how large the pieces are. A full dress shirt or a hoodie gives me
plenty to work with. Smaller garments like baby onesies may need to be
combined with one or two other pieces.
One of the questions I get most often is whether multiple bears can be
made from the same collection of clothing. In many cases, yes. If there
is enough fabric, or if you bring several garments, I can make bears
for more than one person from the same set of clothes. This is a
popular option for families who each want their own keepsake from a
parent or grandparent's wardrobe. Bring what you have to the
consultation and we will look at it together.

How the Memory Bear Process Works

I want you to feel good about every step of this before anything is
cut. Step one: we talk first. Schedule a free consultation and we will
discuss the garment or garments, what this project means to you, and
what you want the finished bear to look and feel like. Nothing is
rushed here.
Step two: send the clothing. You can drop off garments in person by

## Blog: What Is Computerized Longarm Quilting?
**URL:** https://www.bigwes.com/quilt-blog/what-is-computerized-longarm-quilting/

Quilting Services · 2026-06-02 · By Wesley Hall
If you have ever finished a quilt top and then stared at it
wondering how on earth you were going to get all three layers
quilted together without a machine the size of your living room, you
are not alone. That question is exactly what brings most home
quilters to a longarm service for the first time. And the follow-up
question I hear almost every time is some version of: "So what does
the computer actually do?"
It is a fair thing to ask. The term "computerized longarm quilting"
sounds technical in a way that can feel a little intimidating. In
practice, though, the concept is pretty straightforward once you see
it explained plainly. That is what I want to do here.
What a Longarm Machine Is
A longarm quilting machine is a large sewing machine mounted on a
steel frame that can be anywhere from ten to fourteen feet long.
Your quilt top, batting, and backing are loaded onto rollers at
either end of the frame and kept under consistent, even tension
across the whole width of the quilt. The machine head moves freely
across that frame on a track, stitching from side to side and top to
bottom.
The key difference between a longarm and a regular domestic sewing
machine is that with a domestic machine, you move the fabric under a
stationary needle. With a longarm, the fabric stays still and the
machine moves. That switch makes it possible to quilt a king-size
top without wrestling hundreds of square inches of fabric through a
small throat space.
The longarm frame keeps the quilt flat and under even tension while the machine head moves across it.
Where the Computer Comes In
A computerized longarm adds a motion-control system to the machine
head. Instead of the quilter guiding the machine by hand across the
frame, the computer drives the machine head along programmed paths,
stitching out a chosen design automatically and repeatably.
The designs themselves are digital files, similar in concept to
embroidery files if you have ever worked with an embroidery machine.
Each file contains thousands of tiny coordinate points that tell the
machine exactly where to move and when to put the needle down. When
the computer runs that file, the machine traces every one of those
points across the quilt surface, stitching the pattern precisely and
consistently from one edge to the other.
What that means in practice is that a pantograph pattern, the
edge-to-edge design that covers a whole quilt in a repeating motif,
comes out with perfectly even spacing and consistent stitch density
whether you are on the first row or the last. No hand fatigue, no
drift, no variation in tension from one section to the next.
The machine head traces every point in the digital design file automatically.
Even stitch density from edge to edge, even on a king-size top.
Edge-to-Edge vs. Custom Quilting
Most home quilters who send their tops to a longarm service start
with edge-to-edge quilting, sometimes called an E2E or an allover
design. The computer runs the same repeating pattern across the
entire quilt from one edge to the other. It is efficient, it looks
beautiful, and it is the most affordable longarm option because the
setup time is predictable.
Custom quilting is a different thing entirely. With custom work, the
quilter programs or guides the machine to stitch different designs
in different areas of the quilt, feathers in the borders, stippling
in the background, custom motifs centered in large blocks. It takes
significantly more time and skill to plan and execute, which is
reflected in the price. But for a special quilt where the quilting
itself is part of the design, custom work can be extraordinary.
For most memory quilts and t-shirt quilts, an edge-to-edge design is
exactly right. The focus of those quilts is the fabrics and the
memories they hold, and a well-chosen allover pattern frames that
without competing with it.
What You Need to Send to a Longarm Quilter
If you are thinking about sending a top out, here is what a longarm
quilter typically needs from you. Your quilt top should be fully
pieced and pressed, with seams as flat as you can get them. Any
loose threads trimmed. Your backing should be cut at least four
inches larger than the top on all sides, so there is enough to load
onto the frame. Batting should be the same, or you can ask your
longarm quilter to source batting for you.
The most common mistake I see from first-time customers is a backing
that is too small. Four inches of overage on each side sounds like a
lot until you see how the frame works. That margin is what lets the
machine quilt all the way to the edges of your top without running
out of fabric on the backing.
Why Home Quilters Choose to Send Their Tops Out
The honest answer for most people is time and equipment. A domestic
machine can absolutely finish a quilt, but maneuvering a large top
through a small throat space is slow, physical work, and getting
consistent tension across a big quilt is genuinely difficult. A lot
of home quilters put enormous hours into their piecing and then feel
like the finishing step lets the quilt down.
Sending the top to a longarm service means you get a professionally
finished quilt without needing to own or operate a machine that
costs anywhere from ten to thirty thousand dollars. You get your top
back bound-ready, flat, and quilted evenly, and you can put your own
time into the next project.
I work with home quilters here in Atlanta and around Georgia
regularly. Some folks drop off locally, and some ship their tops to
me. Either way, the first conversation is always free, and I am
happy to talk through design options, batting choices, and
turnaround time before you commit to anything.

## Blog: The Color and Design Principles Behind Every T-Shirt Quilt Layout
**URL:** https://www.bigwes.com/quilt-blog/tshirt-quilt-layout-design-principles/

Quilting Services · 2026-05-19 · By Wesley Hall
When a stack of shirts arrives at my studio, the sewing is honestly
the easy part. The real work, the part that determines whether a
finished quilt feels cohesive and intentional or just like a grid of
random squares, is the layout. Every quilt I make goes through a
deliberate design process before a single seam gets sewn, and I want
to walk you through exactly what that looks like.
Step One: Standardizing the Blocks
The first thing I do with any pile of shirts is find the graphic
sizes. T-shirt graphics vary wildly, a band tee might have a huge
chest print, while a 5K race shirt might have a small logo over the
pocket. I look at each graphic and determine the largest block size
that lets me capture every design fully without cropping anything
important.
Once I have that anchor size, I cut all the blocks to match. This is
what gives the finished quilt its clean, structured look. Every
block the same size means the grid lines up perfectly and nothing
feels out of proportion.
Shirts laid out and ready for cutting, finding the right block size is the first design decision.
After cutting, I photograph every individual block. This is where
the real layout work begins. Having photos of each block lets me
arrange and rearrange them digitally, and this is where I often
bring in AI to help me think through the combinations before I
commit to anything.
Individual blocks after cutting, each one photographed so I can work through the layout before touching the sewing machine.
Color Distribution: No Clumping
The most common layout problem I see, and the thing I work hardest
to avoid, is color clumping. If all the red shirts end up in the
bottom right corner and all the blue shirts drift to the top left,
the quilt looks unbalanced even if you can't immediately put your
finger on why.
My goal is to spread each color family evenly across the entire
grid. Think of it like planting a garden, you wouldn't put all your
red flowers in one bed and all your yellow ones in another. You want
color echoing across the whole surface, so your eye naturally
travels around the quilt rather than getting stuck in one spot.
With larger quilts, this can get complex fast. When I'm working
through a 40 or 50 shirt quilt, I'll often use Claude AI to help me
think through the distribution systematically, describing the color
of each block and asking for arrangement suggestions that keep
similar colors separated. It's a genuinely useful tool for that kind
of combinatorial problem.
Visual Weight and Busy vs. Simple Blocks
Color is only part of the equation. Visual weight matters just as
much. A shirt with a large, bold graphic, lots of detail, high
contrast, maybe a dark background, carries more visual weight than a
simple text-only shirt on a light background. If you cluster all the
heavy blocks together, that area of the quilt will feel dense and
overwhelming, while the rest feels empty.
I deliberately alternate heavy and light blocks throughout the
layout. A bold graphic gets a quieter neighbor. A busy, multi-color
design sits next to something simpler. This rhythm is what makes a
finished quilt feel intentional rather than chaotic, even when
you're working with 30 very different shirts from 30 very different
moments in someone's life.
Neighbor Contrast: What Sits Next to What
Beyond overall distribution, I look carefully at every pair of
adjacent blocks. Two dark navy shirts next to each other can
visually merge into one big dark blob. Two shirts with very similar
graphic styles side by side can feel repetitive. On the other end,
two extremely high-contrast blocks next to each other can create a
jarring visual tension that pulls your attention in an uncomfortable
way.
The goal is good contrast between neighbors without visual clash.
Each block should read as its own distinct square while still
flowing naturally into the ones around it. Getting this right across
an entire grid, where changing one block's position affects four
neighbors at once, is genuinely a puzzle, and it's satisfying when
it comes together.
Dark/Light Rhythm Across the Quilt
Zooming out from individual neighbors, I also look at the overall
dark-to-light rhythm of the whole quilt. A well-laid-out quilt has a
kind of visual breathing to it, darker areas give way to lighter
areas, which give way to darker areas again. This creates movement
and flow. A quilt that's uniformly dark feels heavy. One that's
uniformly light feels washed out. The rhythm between them is what
gives the finished piece life.
This is another area where looking at the layout from a distance, or
reviewing the digital arrangement, is really helpful. Up close
you're evaluating individual pairs. From a distance you can see the
overall value pattern across the whole surface.
Graphic Size Variation and Thematic Grouping
Even with standardized block sizes, graphics themselves vary in how
much of the block they fill. A shirt with a giant chest print feels
visually larger than one with a small left-chest logo, even if the
blocks are identical dimensions. I try to mix these throughout the
layout so the quilt feels dynamic, large bold graphics alternating
with smaller, more modest ones.
Sometimes clients ask about grouping shirts thematically, all the
sports shirts together, all the college shirts together, all the
concert tees in a row. Occasionally this works beautifully,
especially if the color palettes within those groups are
complementary. But more often, mixing them produces a better result.
The variety keeps your eye moving and makes the quilt feel like a
full story rather than a series of chapters.
That said, I always follow the client's lead. If grouping by era or
event is meaningful to you, we can absolutely make that work, I'll
just apply all the same contrast and weight principles within each
section.
Why This Process Matters for Your Quilt
All of this, the photography, the digital arrangement, the color
analysis, happens before I sew a single seam. It adds time to the
process, but it's time well spent. A t-shirt quilt is a permanent
object. It's going to hang on a wall or live on a bed for decades.
Getting the layout right means you'll love looking at it for all of
that time, not just in the first few weeks after you receive it.
When you work with me, you're not just getting someone to cut and
sew your shirts together. You're getting a deliberate design process
applied to something that matters to you. That's what makes the
difference between a quilt that's functional and one that's
genuinely beautiful.

Most people who reach out to me about a memory quilt start

What Clothing Carries
Fabric holds things in ways that other materials do not.
A photograph is static. A video is a performance. But clothing

into it over time. The collar of a well-worn dress shirt has
the softness of thousands of mornings. The knees of a pair of
jeans have the crease of however that person moved through
the world.
People talk about this without always naming it. They say a
garment still smells like someone. They say they cannot bring
themselves to wash it. They say it just feels wrong to get
rid of. What they are describing is the way fabric absorbs
presence, not metaphorically, but literally. Worn clothing
is a record of a body, a life, a pattern of days.
A memory quilt does not remove that. It transforms it. The
garment changes form but the fabric is the same fabric. The
story is still in it.
What Makes a Memory Quilt Different
There are many ways to memorialize someone or preserve a
chapter of life. Photos, shadow boxes, scrapbooks, framed
pieces. What separates a quilt from those options is that
a quilt is not displayed. It is used.
Clients describe reaching for their memory quilt during
specific moments, a cold night, a hard day, a holiday
when someone's absence is felt sharply. The quilt does
something a framed photo cannot do. It can be held. It
provides warmth. It has weight. It is present in the room
in a way that participates in daily life rather than
observing it from a wall.
One client told me that sleeping under her quilt felt like
being tucked in again. That sentence has stayed with me.
The quilt did not replace the person she lost. It preserved
connection in a form that could still be felt.
The Process Matters
A memory quilt requires intention at every step. Each piece
of fabric is cut by hand. Each block is placed deliberately.
The layout is designed to reflect the life behind the clothing,
not to fill a grid. When you commission a custom memory quilt,
you are not paying for fabric and thread alone. You are paying
for time, for skill, and for the attention it takes to do this
work carefully.
The consultation is where this starts. Before anything is
cut, we talk. About the person, the garments, what you want
preserved, and how you want the finished quilt to feel. That
conversation shapes every decision that follows.
For Families Moving Through Loss
Memory quilts made after a loss occupy a particular place
in the work I do. These projects are tender, and I approach
them accordingly. There is no rush. The garments are handled
with care. Nothing is altered without your knowledge.
What I have seen repeatedly is that a memory quilt allows
families to move forward without the feeling that they are
leaving someone behind. The clothes do not disappear. They
change form and remain part of everyday life, folded at the
foot of a bed, pulled out on hard nights, passed eventually
to the next generation who will know the person through the
fabric.
Grief has no timeline, and a memory quilt respects that.
It can be folded away when space is needed and pulled close
when comfort is needed. There is no right or wrong way to
use it.
