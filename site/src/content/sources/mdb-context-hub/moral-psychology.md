# Moral Psychology

The descriptive science of how people judge right, wrong, and **fair** — and why
those judgments so often refuse to move when you hand someone a better argument.
This skill explains **what is happening in the moral machinery** so you can
diagnose a values clash, predict whether "this isn't fair" is really about the
outcome or the process, and notice when a team is talking itself into conduct it
would normally reject.

It is **descriptive, not prescriptive.** It tells you how moral cognition works;
it never tells you what is actually right (see Theme 7). And it is the *moral*
lens specifically — for the neighboring lenses, see **When to use something else**.

## When this skill fires

Reach for it when the question is about **rightness, values, or fairness**, not
tactics:

- "Two stakeholders agree on every fact and *still* won't converge — why?"
- "The customer keeps saying it's *unfair*, but the number is objectively market-rate."
- "I've made the rational case three times and the champion won't budge."
- "They're treating a victimless change as a moral betrayal. Where's the victim?"
- "This decision is mathematically right but feels viscerally wrong to everyone."
- "Is our team rationalizing something we shouldn't be doing?"

## The map: four questions decide which lens

1. **Which value is in play?** → Moral Foundations / Morality-as-Cooperation (T1)
2. **Why won't reasoning move them?** → Social Intuitionist Model (T2); dual-process (T3)
3. **Who is the harmed party in their head?** → Theory of Dyadic Morality (T4)
4. **Is this fair — and are we rationalizing?** → Organizational Justice (T6); moral disengagement (T5)

Theme 7 (is–ought) bounds what you may *ethically do* with any of the above.

## 1. Moral Foundations Theory — *which* value governs the call

Moral judgments run on a few intuitive foundations: **Care/harm,
Fairness/cheating, Loyalty/betrayal, Authority/subversion, Sanctity(purity)/
degradation**, plus a proposed **Liberty/oppression**. They cluster into
**individualizing** (care + fairness — protect the person) vs **binding** (loyalty
+ authority + sanctity — protect the group). Best-replicated result: a **political
asymmetry** — progressives lean on care + fairness; conservatives weight all
roughly equally; libertarians weight liberty highest. Measured by the MFQ → MFQ-2.

**Contested — say so.** Factor analysis frequently recovers only **two** clean
factors, not five, and the rival **Morality-as-Cooperation** (Curry 2019) derives
**seven** cooperative domains from game theory and claims better grounding. Treat
MFT as a **working vocabulary**, not settled fact.

> **Operator application.** A values-based stakeholder conflict is usually a fight
> over **which foundation applies** (a "fairness" framing vs a "we honor our
> commitments / loyalty" framing), not over the facts. **Name the foundation in
> play** instead of adding more evidence — evidence aimed at the wrong foundation
> slides off.

## 2. Social Intuitionist Model — intuition first, reasoning is the press secretary

Haidt 2001: moral judgment is **fast intuition first**; conscious reasoning is
mostly **post-hoc justification** — the "press secretary, not the judge." But the
model is *social*: reasoned persuasion and others' stances **can** reshape a
person's intuition (links 3/5/6), so reasoning has **limited but real** force —
mostly through people and over time, not by winning the argument in the moment.
Evidence: **moral dumbfounding** ("it's wrong, I just can't say why" — the Julie &
Mark vignette). **Caveat:** the original dumbfounding study was an unpublished,
**n≈30** demonstration; later work supports the phenomenon but debates its rate.

> **Operator application.** When **purely rational arguments bounce off**, it's
> because the intuition came first and reason is defending it. **Reframe the
> intuition** (engage a different gut read) or use **social/relational channels**
> (a trusted peer moves the intuition more than your logic does) rather than
> arguing harder.

## 3. Dual-Process Moral Judgment — the emotional vs cognitive split

Greene: an **automatic/emotional** route pushes toward **deontological** judgments
("it's just wrong to push him"), a **controlled/cognitive** route toward
**utilitarian** ones ("5 > 1"). **Trolley/switch** (impersonal) → most permit;
**footbridge** (personal, hands-on) → most refuse, despite identical math.
Cognitive load **selectively slows utilitarian** judgments; VMPFC-lesion patients
are **more utilitarian**.

**Contested — attach the caveats.** Kahane 2015: sacrificial "utilitarian" answers
track **reduced harm-aversion (Instrumental Harm)**, *not* genuine **Impartial
Beneficence** (Oxford Utilitarianism Scale) — so "more utilitarian on trolley" is
not "more moral." The **CNI model** (Gawronski 2017) shows the dichotomy conflates
Consequences-sensitivity, Norms-sensitivity, and a general **Inaction** bias.
Trolley vignettes also draw heavy **ecological-validity** criticism.

> **Operator application.** When a decision is mathematically sound but feels
> viscerally wrong (a cold-formula layoff, a technically-fair clawback), expect
> **deontological recoil** that no spreadsheet beats. Reduce the
> *personalness/directness* of the harm, or wrap it in fair process (Theme 6) —
> don't just re-present the arithmetic.

## 4. Theory of Dyadic Morality — find the victim in their head

Schein & Gray 2018: moral cognition is organized by a template — an **intentional
Agent harming a vulnerable Patient (A→P)**. Two phenomena: **moral typecasting**
(agents and patients seen as opposites — villains feel less pain, victims can do
no wrong) and **dyadic completion** (for a "victimless" wrong, people **invent a
victim** — society, the relationship, future users, their own integrity).
**Contested vs MFT** on whether *all* morality reduces to harm — unresolved.

> **Operator application.** When a stakeholder frames a **victimless** change as a
> moral breach, they've already cast **someone as harmed** (their team, their
> users, the relationship) and **you as the intentional agent**. Surface **who
> they perceive as harmed** before debating intent — "no one was hurt" just gets
> the template re-completed.

## 5. Moral Licensing & Moral Disengagement — how people excuse bad acts

**Moral licensing** (Monin & Miller 2001): a past good act licenses a later
questionable one. **It is small and fragile — state this plainly:** meta-analytic
**d≈0.31** with strong publication-bias signals → bias-corrected to **d≈0.18 or
near null**, and a large registered replication found ≈zero. **Do not build a plan
around inducing it.**

**Moral disengagement** (Bandura 1999) is the actionable one — **eight mechanisms**
for switching off self-sanction, used as a **detection checklist**: ① moral
justification ② euphemistic labeling ③ advantageous comparison ④ displacement of
responsibility ⑤ diffusion of responsibility ⑥ distortion/disregard of
consequences ⑦ dehumanization ⑧ attribution of blame.

> **Operator application.** When a team slides toward conduct it would normally
> reject (misleading a customer, burying a known defect), listen for these eight
> verbal tells. **Naming the mechanism** out loud ("that's euphemistic labeling —
> say what we're actually doing") re-engages the self-sanction the language was
> suppressing.

## 6. Organizational Justice — fairness is process + treatment, not just outcome

The highest-impact theme. Colquitt 2001's **four dimensions**:
**distributive** (the outcome — equity theory; equity/equality/need norms),
**procedural** (the process — Leventhal's six rules: **consistency,
bias-suppression, accuracy, correctability, representativeness, ethicality** +
**voice**), **interpersonal** (dignity and respect), and **informational** (honest,
adequate explanations).

**The fair-process effect** is the single most useful finding: a **fair process
raises acceptance of even an unfavorable outcome.** People accept a price increase,
a denied feature, or a lost escalation they dislike **if** the process was
consistent, gave them **voice**, treated them with **dignity**, and was **honestly
explained** — and resent a *good* outcome delivered arbitrarily.

> **Operator application.** "This isn't fair" is rarely only about the dollar
> amount (distributive). It's usually **process** (no warning, no voice,
> inconsistent with last time) and **treatment** (curt, no real explanation). You
> often can't change the outcome — but you can almost always improve **process and
> explanation**: advance notice, voice, consistent rule, candid reasons, an appeal
> path. That is the lever that turns "unfair" into "I don't love it, but I get it"
> — in pricing changes, escalations, negotiations, and missed-SLA conversations.

## 7. Is–Ought / Naturalistic Fallacy — the limit on ethical nudging

Everything above is **descriptive**. **Hume**: you cannot derive an *ought* from an
*is*. **Moore** (*Principia Ethica*, open-question argument): "good" is not
definable by any natural property. Describing how moral cognition works **never
prescribes** what is right — disgust driving purity judgments doesn't make them
correct, and fairness perception being hackable via process doesn't make it
ethical to hack.

> **Operator application.** This bounds the **ethics of nudging**: use moral
> psychology to **understand and communicate honestly**, not to manufacture
> consent or steer people past their own judgment. When someone argues "it's
> natural, therefore it's fine," flag the is→ought gap.

This skill owns the **moral judgment / fairness-perception** layer: which value is
in play, why reason won't move a moral stance, who is cast as harmed, whether a
process is *perceived* as fair, and whether a team is rationalizing.

## A reusable diagnostic prompt

Paste this when a values/fairness clash is stuck:

```
Act as a moral-psychology analyst. For the situation below, work through:
1. FOUNDATION — which moral foundation(s) is each party invoking
   (care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, liberty)? Is the conflict a
   foundation mismatch rather than a factual disagreement?
2. INTUITION vs REASON — is a fast moral intuition driving this, with reasoning
   as post-hoc justification? Will more logic move it, or do I need to reframe
   the intuition or route through a trusted peer?
3. DYADIC TEMPLATE — who is cast as the harmed Patient and who as the
   intentional Agent? If it looks "victimless," who have they completed the
   dyad with?
4. FAIRNESS — separate distributive (outcome) from procedural (consistency,
   voice, accuracy, correctability), interpersonal (dignity), and informational
   (honest explanation). Which is actually driving "this isn't fair"? What
   fair-process move raises acceptance without changing the outcome?
5. DISENGAGEMENT CHECK — are we (my side) using any of Bandura's 8 mechanisms
   (moral justification, euphemistic labeling, advantageous comparison,
   displacement/diffusion of responsibility, distortion of consequences,
   dehumanization, attribution of blame) to rationalize our position?
6. ETHICAL LIMIT — is any proposed move an is→ought overreach — exploiting the
   machinery to override informed consent rather than to communicate honestly?
Flag replication caveats (MFT factor structure, dumbfounding origin, licensing
fragility, Kahane/CNI critique of "utilitarian" answers) where they apply.
Situation: <DESCRIBE THE VALUES OR FAIRNESS CONFLICT>
```
