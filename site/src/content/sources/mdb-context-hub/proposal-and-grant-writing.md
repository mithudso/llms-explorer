# Proposal and Grant Writing

## Overview

Grant proposals are a constrained writing genre. Reviewers read against a rubric, in a hurry, under fatigue. A proposal that "reads well" but ignores the funder's stated criteria scores low. A proposal that follows the rubric mechanically but tells a coherent change story scores high.

## Core Concepts

### 1. Problem statement anchor

Three reviewer-tested moves:
1. **Open in the funder's frame.** Quote the funding opportunity's priority area in the first two sentences.
2. **Quantify the problem at the right scope.** National statistics are weak without local data; local data is weak without national context.
3. **End on the gap.** Close with the specific knowledge, service, or capacity gap your project will fill.

### 2. Demonstrated need (vs. felt need)

"We need money" is a felt need. Demonstrated need is evidence — a quantified disparity, an unmet target, a documented service desert.

Pull from peer-reviewed literature, government data (Census, BLS, CDC, HRSA), and community needs assessments. Avoid undated statistics; reviewers flag anything older than 5 years.

### 3. Theory of change as the red thread

A theory of change is the narrative map of how change happens: if we do X (activities), given Y (assumptions), then Z (outcomes), because of M (mechanism). Reviewers check whether the aims, methods, and evaluation plan trace back to the theory of change.

### 4. Logframe (logical framework matrix)

| Level | Description | Indicators | Means of Verification | Assumptions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Goal | Long-term impact | Impact indicators | Population data | External conditions |
| Outcome | Behavior/condition change | Outcome indicators | Survey, administrative data | Stakeholder cooperation |
| Output | Direct deliverables | Output indicators | Activity logs | Resource availability |
| Activity | What you do | Process indicators | Workplans, timesheets | Staffing capacity |

**Critical distinction:** **outputs are not outcomes**. "Conducted 100 workshops" is an output. "Participants reduced X behavior by Y%" is an outcome.

### 5. SMART objectives

Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound.

**Weak:** "Improve youth literacy."
**SMART:** "By June 2027, increase the proportion of 4th-grade students at our three partner schools reading at grade level from 42% to 60%, as measured by the state ELA assessment."

### 6. NIH Specific Aims four-paragraph structure

**One page. Four paragraphs:**
1. **Hook + significance.** Open with the broad problem. Narrow to the specific gap.
2. **Central hypothesis + rationale.** State your hypothesis. Explain why it is plausible. Establish that you are the right team.
3. **Specific Aims.** 2-4 aims. Each aim is independent. Use confident verbs: "We will determine," not "We hope to."
4. **Payoff.** State the expected outcomes and their impact on the field.

### 7. Budget narrative

Each cost category (personnel, fringe, travel, equipment, supplies, contractual, indirect) gets a paragraph answering: what, why, how calculated, why this rate.

**Reviewer red flags:** round numbers without basis, salaries above the federal cap, equipment without quotes.

### 8. Sustainability plan

Strong sustainability plans show:
- Diversified funding (other grants secured or pending, earned revenue, in-kind commitments)
- Institutionalization (policy adoption, embedded staff lines)
- Capacity built (trained personnel, data systems)

**Weak:** "We will pursue additional funding."

### 9. Executive summary as standalone document

Must contain: the problem, the proposed solution, the requested amount and duration, the lead organization's qualifications, expected outcomes, and the sustainability commitment. Roughly 250-500 words. Write it last.

### 10. Attachments hygiene

Every attachment listed in the funding opportunity must be present, named exactly as specified, in the format specified (PDF most often), within the page limit.

## Templates

### Problem statement template

> [Funder priority area, quoted or paraphrased from the FOA]. [National-scale statistic with citation, ≤5 years old]. In [target geography], the problem is [more acute] because [local data point]. Despite [existing efforts], [the specific gap]. This proposal addresses [the gap] through [one-sentence approach preview].

### NIH Specific Aims skeleton

```
[Para 1: Significance]
[Problem context — 2 sentences]. [Narrow to gap — 2 sentences].

[Para 2: Hypothesis + team]
[Central hypothesis — 1 sentence]. [Rationale from preliminary data + literature — 2-3 sentences].

[Para 3: Aims]
Aim 1: Determine [X]. Hypothesis: [Y]. Approach: [Z].
Aim 2: Characterize [X]. Hypothesis: [Y]. Approach: [Z].

[Para 4: Payoff]
The expected outcome is [specific deliverable]. This will [impact on field].
```

## Anti-Patterns

1. **Activity list disguised as a proposal.** Listing workshops without tying each to an outcome.
2. **Outputs masquerading as outcomes.** "We will train 500 people" is not an outcome.
3. **Generic problem statement.** Opening with a global statistic with no connection to the funder priority.
4. **Hedge language in aims.** "We hope to," "we will attempt to." Use "We will determine."
5. **Sustainability as boilerplate.** "We will pursue additional funding."
