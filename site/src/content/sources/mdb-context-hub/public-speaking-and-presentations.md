# Public Speaking and Presentations

## Audience Analysis Before You Open PowerPoint

Answer four questions before touching a slide tool:

1. **Who is in the room?** Job function, seniority, and domain expertise.
2. **What do they already believe?** Start from their current position.
3. **What one action do you want them to take?** Name the action.
4. **What is the one thing they must remember?** Write that sentence before slide 1.

## Slide-Doc vs Presentation Slides

**Presentation slides** (Reynolds / "Presentation Zen" model) exist to support a live speaker. The speaker carries the argument; the slide carries visual reinforcement. One idea per slide. Minimal text. Large images or diagrams.

**Slide-docs** (Tufte-influenced) are standalone documents formatted as slide-shaped pages. They contain complete sentences, dense supporting data, footnotes. They can be emailed and read without a presenter.

**The core failure mode:** the slidument — bullet-heavy slides that are too dense to look good on screen and too sparse to be useful to a reader.

## Kawasaki 10/20/30 Rule

- **10 slides** — humans can't absorb more than ten concepts in a single sitting
- **20 minutes** — even if you have a full hour, present in 20 minutes
- **30-point minimum font** — if you cannot fit your message in 30pt type, you have too many words

## Nancy Duarte's Resonate Framework

The central insight: every great speech moves back and forth between "what is" and "what could be."

**The contrast pattern:**
1. Establish the current reality (what is)
2. Describe the future possibility (what could be)
3. Repeat this oscillation; each cycle raises the stakes
4. End with the "new bliss" — the world after the audience acts

**The STAR moment** (Something They'll Always Remember): one designed moment of high contrast, surprise, or visceral demonstration.

## Three-Act Talk Structure

**Act 1 — Setup (10–15% of total time):** Establish stakes; define the problem; signal what the audience will be able to do differently by the end.

**Act 2 — Exploration (70–75%):** Develop the argument. Front-load complex material; place a re-energizing moment two-thirds of the way through.

**Act 3 — Resolution (10–15%):** Do not introduce new material. Synthesize, do not summarize.

## The Headline-Deck Approach (McKinsey / BCG)

Every slide title is a complete, standalone sentence stating the takeaway, not a topic label.

- Bad title: "Market Analysis"
- Good title: "The North American market is growing 12% YoY and outpacing EMEA in every segment."

A reader who reads only slide titles should follow the full argument.

## Talk Openings — Five Patterns

1. **Startling fact or statistic:** drop a number the audience does not expect
2. **Story:** open with a specific scene — one person, one moment, sensory detail
3. **Contrarian claim:** state something the audience believes that you are about to challenge
4. **Direct question:** ask a question the audience cannot immediately answer
5. **Scene-setting:** describe the world as it will look after your idea is adopted

Do not open with: your name and title, an apology, "great to be here," agenda slides.

## Talk Closings — Five Patterns

1. **Callback to opening:** return to the story or question you opened with
2. **Call to action:** one specific, concrete thing the audience can do in the next 48 hours
3. **Single image or quote:** full-bleed slide with one sentence
4. **Summary triple:** three short phrases that encapsulate the talk's three main points
5. **The future vision:** describe the world three years from now if the audience acts

## Q&A Handling

**The bridging technique:** acknowledge the question, then bridge to your main message.

**"I don't know" as a complete answer:** say it cleanly, then add "I'll find out and get back to you by [date]."

**Hostile questions:** do not match the energy. Lower your voice slightly. Answer the question beneath the question.

## Rehearsal Discipline

Minimum effective rehearsal for a 30-minute talk:
1. **Three times out loud, alone** — full volume, full sentences, full transitions
2. **Once to a real human** — their confusion is signal
3. **Video review of yourself** — watch on mute first (body language), then with sound (pace and filler)

**Pacing target:** 125–150 words per minute. Time your talk at 90% of the slot.

## Virtual vs In-Person Calibration

**Virtual:** look at the camera lens, not at faces on screen. Place the camera at eye level.

**Audio quality (virtual):** audio quality matters more than video quality. A USB condenser microphone, a quiet room, and a soft surface behind you.

**Energy calibration (virtual):** increase vocal variety noticeably above what feels natural in a room — the camera flattens affect.

## Anti-Patterns to Eliminate

- **Reading slides verbatim.**
- **Walls of bullets.** Each bullet resets the audience's attention to near-zero.
- **"As you can see here..."** — If it is visible, do not narrate its existence.
- **Apologizing for slides.**
- **Running long.** Going over time disrespects every person in the room.
- **The vague closing.** "So, in conclusion, we looked at X, Y, and Z..."
