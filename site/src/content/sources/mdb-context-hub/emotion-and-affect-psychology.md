# Emotion & Affect Psychology

The evidence-based science of **emotion**: what it is, how it arises, how it
spreads, and how to regulate it — applied to two operator problems:

1. **The customer's emotion**: de-escalating a furious stakeholder, reading the
   mood of a room, not getting infected by a tense call.
2. **Your own emotion**: staying regulated in an escalation, not letting a bad
   mood color a judgment, not over-dreading a hard conversation.

This skill owns emotion **mechanics**. Where emotion shades into a *syndrome*
(chronic stress, burnout, resilience) it hands off to
`performance-and-resilience-psychology`; where it shades into *moving someone*
(persuasion, trust, adoption) it hands off to the relevant sibling. The handoff
lines are in the SKIP block above — honor them so the family stays non-redundant.

A note on honesty: emotion science is **contested and partly unreplicated**.
There is no agreed-upon list of "the emotions". The most popular applied advice
("name it to tame it", "EQ predicts success", "always reappraise") is each
either oversold or context-dependent. This skill flags every one of those.

## The eight mechanisms

| # | Mechanism | One-line claim | Operator use |
|---|-----------|----------------|--------------|
| 1 | **Appraisal theory** (Lazarus; Scherer CPM) | Emotion comes from your *interpretation* of an event, not the event | Change the appraisal → change the emotion (yours or theirs) |
| 2 | **Structure of emotion** (Ekman / Russell / Barrett) | There is no settled list of emotions; basic vs dimensional vs constructed all compete | Don't over-trust facial "reads"; use valence×arousal as a working frame |
| 3 | **Emotion regulation** (Gross process model) | Five families of regulation; reappraisal usually beats suppression (but not always) | A concrete menu of where to intervene, before vs after the feeling fires |
| 4 | **Affect-as-information** (Schwarz & Clore) | "How do I feel about it?" mood gets misread as data about the object | Catch when your mood, not the facts, is driving a judgment |
| 5 | **Affective forecasting** (Gilbert & Wilson) | You overestimate how long/hard a future event will feel (impact/durability bias) | Stop over-dreading the hard call; calibrate the customer's catastrophizing |
| 6 | **Emotional contagion** (Hatfield, Cacioppo, Rapson) | You automatically catch other people's emotions via mimicry | Manage what you radiate; notice when you've caught the room's panic |
| 7 | **Emotional intelligence** (Mayer-Salovey ability model) | A real but modest ability to perceive/use/understand/manage emotion: oversold by pop models | Train the four branches; ignore the "EQ > IQ" hype |
| 8 | **Emotional labor** (Hochschild) | Managing feeling for a wage; surface acting (faking) costs more than deep acting | Pick deep acting over surface acting on hard calls; watch the exhaustion bridge |

## 1. Appraisal theory — emotion is about meaning, not events

The foundational insight (**Lazarus**): an event does not cause an emotion
directly. Your **appraisal** of the event does. The same outage makes one
customer curious ("interesting, let's dig in") and another furious ("this will
get me fired") — because they appraise it differently. This is *the* lever:
**you cannot always change the event, but you can almost always influence the
appraisal.**

Lazarus split appraisal into two steps:

- **Primary appraisal**: *Is this relevant to my goals, and is it good or bad?*
  The event is classified as **harm/loss** (damage done), **threat** (harm
  coming), or **challenge** (a hard but winnable opportunity). Threat and
  challenge can attach to the *same* situation — the difference is largely in
  the appraiser.
- **Secondary appraisal**: *Can I cope with it? What are my resources and
  options?* High perceived coping → challenge emotions (engagement, determination).
  Low perceived coping → threat emotions (anxiety, dread, anger).

**Scherer's Component Process Model (CPM)** formalizes appraisal into a sequence
of **Stimulus Evaluation Checks** run very fast and roughly in order:

1. **Relevance**: novelty, intrinsic pleasantness, goal relevance.
2. **Implication**: causal attribution (who/what caused it), goal conduciveness,
   urgency, outcome probability.
3. **Coping potential**: control, power, ability to adjust.
4. **Normative significance**: does it fit my standards, values, self-concept?

CPM treats emotion as a **process**, not a fixed state: the checks unfold over
time, and the emotion is the synchronized pattern across components (cognition,
physiology, action tendency, expression, feeling). It is the most detailed and
most testable appraisal model.

**Operator moves (appraisal is the master lever):**
- **Re-appraise the customer's event toward "challenge" and toward high coping.**
  "This is a bad week, *and* it's a configuration issue we know how to fix, and
  here's the plan" attacks both the primary (threat→challenge) and the secondary
  (low→high coping) appraisal. You are not denying the problem; you are changing
  what it *means*.
- **Diagnose which appraisal is driving the heat.** Anger usually follows an
  *other-blame* attribution + a *should-not-have-happened* normative check. Anxiety
  follows *low control / low coping*. Naming the driver tells you what to address:
  attribution ("here's what actually happened, and it wasn't you") for anger,
  control ("here are the three levers you still have") for anxiety.

> Appraisal also underpins the stress literature (Lazarus & Folkman's
> challenge-vs-threat). That stress/coping framing lives in
> `performance-and-resilience-psychology`; this skill owns appraisal as the
> general engine of *all* emotion, not just stress.

## 2. The structure of emotion — three competing models, no winner

There is **no scientific consensus on what emotions fundamentally are**. Three
camps, often described as fighting a "**hundred years' war**":

**(a) Basic / discrete emotions (Ekman, Tomkins).** A small set of biologically
basic emotions — classically **anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness,
surprise**: each with (allegedly) a universal facial signal, a distinct
physiology, and a dedicated adaptive function. This is the model behind "read the
microexpression" training.
*Caveat — this is the most empirically challenged claim in the field.* The
"universal facial expression" link is weak in real-life (non-posed) emotion;
context and culture dominate the read. **Do not bet a customer call on reading
someone's true feeling off their face.**

**(b) Dimensional / circumplex (Russell).** Emotions aren't discrete categories
but points in a continuous space defined by two axes: **valence** (pleasant↔
unpleasant) and **arousal** (activated↔deactivated). "Tense" = negative valence,
high arousal; "calm" = positive valence, low arousal; "bored" = negative, low.
This is the most *useful working frame for operators* because it is
action-guiding (see below).

**(c) Theory of constructed emotion (Barrett).** Emotions are not triggered;
they are **constructed** in the moment by the brain, which makes meaning of raw
**core affect** (the basic valence+arousal feeling) using learned **emotion
concepts** shaped by culture and language. There is no fingerprint for "anger" in
the face, body, or brain — "anger" is a category your brain applies, not a thing
it detects. Implication: emotions vary far more between people and cultures than
basic-emotion theory assumes, and **emotional granularity** (having fine-grained
concepts) is a trainable skill that improves regulation.

**Operator working model — use the dimensional frame, hold the rest loosely:**
- Map the customer onto **valence × arousal** in real time. Your job in a heated
  call is almost always **arousal-down first, valence-up second**: a furious
  customer (negative valence, *high* arousal) cannot absorb a solution until
  arousal drops. Solve the arousal axis (slow down, lower your voice, give them
  room to vent) before you try to move valence (the fix, the apology).
- **Build emotional granularity** (Barrett's practical payoff): the more precisely
  you can label what you and the customer feel ("this is *frustration about being
  unheard*", not just "they're mad"), the better you regulate and respond. Coarse
  labels → coarse responses.
- **Distrust facial reads.** Treat expression as a weak, context-dependent cue,
  not ground truth. Verify with words.

## 3. Emotion regulation — Gross's process model (the core toolkit)

**James Gross's process model** is the single most useful framework here. It maps
*where in the emotion-generation timeline* you can intervene. Emotion unfolds as
**Situation → Attention → Appraisal → Response**, and there is a regulation
family at each point. The first four are **antecedent-focused** (act *before* the
emotion is in full swing); the last is **response-focused** (act *after*).

| Family | When | What it is | Operator example |
|--------|------|-----------|------------------|
| **1. Situation selection** | Before | Choose which situations to enter/avoid | Take the angry call when *you're* regulated, not right after another bad one |
| **2. Situation modification** | Before | Change the situation itself | Move a hostile thread to a live call; bring in the right expert; change the setting |
| **3. Attentional deployment** | During | Shift what you attend to | Refocus a spiraling customer onto the one fixable thing; distraction/grounding for yourself |
| **4. Cognitive change (reappraisal)** | During | Reinterpret the situation's meaning | Reframe "they're attacking me" → "they're scared about their job" (lowers *your* anger) |
| **5. Response modulation (suppression)** | After | Change the emotional response once it's up | Keep a neutral face/voice while internally rattled (deep breathing also lives here) |

### Reappraisal usually beats suppression — but it's conditional

The most-cited finding: **cognitive reappraisal is generally more adaptive than
expressive suppression.**
- **Reappraisal** (acting early, on meaning) lowers the *experience* and the
  *expression* of negative emotion, with little physiological cost and **no memory
  cost**. Neuroimaging (Buhle et al., 2014, meta-analysis of ~48 studies) shows
  reappraisal recruits prefrontal control regions and **down-regulates the
  amygdala**.
- **Suppression** (acting late, on expression) leaves the *inner* negative feeling
  largely intact, **increases** physiological activation (sympathetic load),
  **impairs memory** for the interaction, and tends to **reduce rapport** —
  suppressors are experienced by partners as less likable and raise the partner's
  blood pressure. It can also blunt *positive* emotion.

**The crucial caveat — reappraisal is not universally good (Troy, Shallcross &
Mauss, 2013).** Adaptiveness is **context-dependent**:
- When the stressor is **uncontrollable** (a customer's company reorg, a decision
  above your pay grade), high reappraisal ability → **lower** depression. Reframing
  is the right tool when you can't change the thing.
- When the stressor is **controllable** (a fixable bug, a process you own), high
  reappraisal ability → **higher** depression. Here, reframing your feelings
  instead of *fixing the problem* is maladaptive — you should change the situation,
  not just reinterpret it.
- The mature skill is **regulatory flexibility** (Bonanno & Burton): match the
  strategy to the context, and switch when feedback says it isn't working — not
  "always reappraise".

### "Name it to tame it" — a popular claim that the lab complicates

Affect labeling (putting feelings into words) is widely promoted as a regulation
tool, and *implicit* labeling does down-regulate the amygdala in some studies.
**But** a 2021 study (Nook et al.) found that **deliberately naming an emotion
before reappraising made people feel *worse*, not better**: naming may
"crystallize" the affective experience into a discrete category and make it
*harder* to reinterpret. Practical read: with a customer, **acknowledging** their
emotion ("I can hear this is really frustrating") is about *rapport and feeling
heard*, which is valuable — but don't assume forcing a precise label on your *own*
feeling will make it easier to reappraise. Sometimes it does the opposite.

**Operator regulation playbook (yours, before and during a hard call):**
1. **Situation selection/modification first**: these are the highest-yield and
   most-ignored. Don't take the call dysregulated; change the channel; stage the
   environment.
2. **In the moment, lead with reappraisal** if the trigger is something you can't
   control ("their anger is about their situation, not about me").
3. **If you CAN fix the thing, fix it**: don't reappraise your way out of a
   problem you should solve (the Troy caveat).
4. **Use response modulation (a slow breath, neutral tone) as a bridge**, knowing
   suppression alone is costly and visible over time. Pair it with deep acting
   (§8), not bare surface suppression.

## 4. Affect-as-information — when feelings get mistaken for facts

**Schwarz & Clore (1983), feelings-as-information theory.** People making an
evaluative judgment often ask, implicitly, **"How do I feel about it?"**: and
read their *current feeling* as information about the *object of judgment*, even
when the feeling actually comes from somewhere else (the weather, hunger, a prior
argument). In the classic study, people rated their **whole life as more
satisfying on sunny days than rainy days**: unless the interviewer first
mentioned the weather, which made them **discount** the mood ("oh, that's just
the rain") and the effect vanished.

Two well-replicated implications:

- **Mood-congruent judgment.** A negative mood makes you evaluate ambiguous things
  more negatively; a positive mood, more positively. The judgment feels like it's
  about the object; it's partly about your affect.
- **Misattribution & discounting.** The effect disappears when the *true source*
  of the feeling becomes salient. Naming the source breaks the illusion.
- **Processing style.** Schwarz & Clore also link mood to *how* you think: **happy
  mood → heuristic, global, top-down** processing (the situation feels safe, coast
  on impressions); **sad mood → analytic, detail-oriented, bottom-up** processing
  (something's wrong, scrutinize). Neither is "better" — they fit different tasks.

**Operator moves:**
- **Don't make a consequential judgment in a strong unrelated mood.** Just lost a
  deal and now reviewing an unrelated account's health? Your read will skew
  negative. Defer, or explicitly ask "is this about the account, or about my day?"
  — naming the source is the documented antidote.
- **Exploit the processing link.** Detail work (reviewing a contract, debugging a
  config) is *better served by a slightly serious/critical mood*; big-picture
  brainstorming and relationship warmth are *better served by a positive mood*.
  Don't force creative rapport-building when you're in a heads-down critical state,
  and vice versa.
- This is the *input* side of how emotion biases cognition. The downstream **bias
  catalog** (risk-as-feelings, affect heuristic in risk perception, prospect
  theory) lives in `behavioral-decision-making`.

## 5. Affective forecasting — you're bad at predicting your own feelings

**Gilbert & Wilson.** People routinely **mispredict** their future emotions — how
intense and especially **how long** a feeling will last. Two named biases:

- **Impact bias**: overestimating the *intensity* of a future emotional reaction.
- **Durability bias**: overestimating the *duration*. This is the bigger, more
  reliably observed error.

Why it happens:
- **Focalism**: you focus on the single triggering event and neglect all the
  *other* things that will also fill your days and dilute the feeling.
- **Immune neglect**: you underestimate your **psychological immune system**: the
  unconscious machinery (rationalizing, reframing, finding silver linings) that
  quietly attenuates negative affect. Because it works *outside awareness*, you
  don't anticipate it — so you over-forecast how bad and how long the bad event
  will feel. Demonstrated across romantic breakups, tenure denial, election losses,
  negative feedback, and job rejection: people predicted long-lasting misery; the
  actual emotional dip was milder and faster-fading than predicted.

**Operator moves:**
- **Stop over-dreading the hard call.** The escalation you're catastrophizing about
  will almost certainly feel less awful, and pass faster, than your forecast says.
  Naming "this is impact/durability bias" lowers the avoidance that makes you
  procrastinate the call.
- **Calibrate the customer's catastrophizing — gently.** A customer convinced that
  an incident "ruins everything" is impact-biased about a *negative* event. You can
  widen the frame (de-focalize): "Six months from now, what will matter is how we
  responded — and here's the response." Don't dismiss the feeling; reframe the
  *durability*.
- **Watch the same bias on the upside.** Customers over-forecast how good a feature/
  migration will make them feel; manage expectations so the post-purchase dip
  (hedonic adaptation) doesn't read as a failure on your part.

## 6. Emotional contagion — you catch the room's mood automatically

**Hatfield, Cacioppo & Rapson (1993/1994), primitive emotional contagion:** "the
tendency to automatically **mimic and synchronize** facial expressions,
vocalizations, postures, and movements with those of another person and,
consequently, **to converge emotionally**." It runs through three stages, mostly
**outside awareness**:

1. **Mimicry**: you unconsciously copy the other person's expression, posture,
   vocal tone, tempo.
2. **Feedback**: your own copied expression/posture feeds back and *induces the
   matching feeling* in you (facial-feedback mechanism).
3. **Contagion**: you converge on their emotional state.

Two findings with direct operator weight:
- People's **conscious** read of how someone feels is driven by **words**; their
  **own caught emotion** is driven by the other person's **nonverbal** cues. So you
  can be told "everything's fine" and still *catch* the underlying tension — trust
  that signal.
- Contagion flows **toward the higher-arousal / more expressive person** and, in
  groups, toward the most emotionally expressive member. A panicked customer can
  infect a whole account team; one calm, grounded operator can also set the tone.

**Operator moves:**
- **Manage what you radiate.** In an escalation you are a contagion *source*. A
  calm, slow, low-arousal vocal pattern is not just for show — it literally
  down-regulates the customer via mimicry→feedback. **Co-regulation:** you lend
  them your calm.
- **Notice when you've been infected.** Mid-call, if your heart is racing and your
  pace has spiked, you may have caught *their* arousal, not generated your own. Name
  it internally ("this is contagion") and deliberately reset pace/posture — that
  breaks the loop.
- **Protect the team.** Don't let a single panicked stakeholder set the emotional
  baseline of a war-room. Seed it with grounded expressive calm.

> This is the *affective* channel between people. The *cognitive/relational*
> channel — trust, rapport, synchrony as a rapport signal — lives in
> `trust-and-psychological-safety`. They overlap at behavioral synchrony; this
> skill owns the emotion-transfer mechanism.

## 7. Emotional intelligence — real but modest, and badly oversold

**Mayer & Salovey's ability model** defines EI as a genuine *intelligence* — a set
of abilities for reasoning with and about emotion, in four branches (in
developmental order):

1. **Perceiving emotion**: identify emotion in faces, voices, yourself.
2. **Using/facilitating**: harness emotion to aid thinking (e.g., use a serious
   mood for detail work — note the tie-in with §4).
3. **Understanding emotion**: know emotion vocabulary, causes, and how emotions
   blend and transition (e.g., that contempt is anger+disgust, that frustration can
   tip to resignation).
4. **Managing emotion**: regulate emotion in yourself and others to reach a goal
   (this branch *is* §3 applied).

Measured by the **MSCEIT**, an ability test scored by **consensus** (your answer
vs a large normative sample) or **expert** scoring (vs 21 emotion researchers).
**Built-in weakness:** there's no objectively correct answer — a response is
"emotionally intelligent" only if most people endorsed it, so the test arguably
measures *conformity to norms* and *knowing* the right move rather than the *ability
to execute* it under fire.

**Two rival families, weaker science:**
- **Mixed models (Goleman)**: self-awareness, self-management/regulation, social
  awareness/empathy, relationship management/social skill. Popular in business,
  but blends genuine abilities with personality traits and motivation.
- **Trait EI (Petrides; Bar-On's EQ-i)**: self-reported emotional self-perceptions;
  sits "at the lower levels of personality" and largely overlaps the Big Five. The
  trait model essentially *subsumes* Goleman's mixed model.

**The honest critique (state this when someone invokes "EQ"):**
- **"EQ matters more than IQ" is not supported.** Meta-analyses: EI correlates with
  job performance at roughly **r ≈ 0.20 (ability EI)** to **r ≈ 0.29 (mixed EI)** —
  modest, not dominant.
- **Incremental validity is the real problem.** When you control for **IQ and the
  Big Five**, **ability-EI's predictive validity drops to ~zero** (2010
  meta-analysis); a 2015 meta-analysis found mixed-EI's contribution to job
  performance "non-existent" after those controls. Self-report/trait measures keep
  *some* validity — partly because they're re-measuring personality. EI's link to
  transformational leadership shrinks to **ρ ≈ 0.11** with method-bias-free data.
- Net: EI is a **real but small** ability, mostly useful as a *trainable skill set*
  (the four branches), not as a magic predictor of success. The branches are worth
  developing; the hype is not.

**Operator moves:** train the branches deliberately — granularity (branch 3) and
managing-others (branch 4) are the highest-yield for customer work. Treat any
vendor "EQ assessment" predicting performance with the skepticism the meta-analyses
warrant.

## 8. Emotional labor — the cost of managing feeling for a wage

**Arlie Hochschild, *The Managed Heart* (1983).** **Emotional labor** is managing
your feelings and expressions as part of the job — the flight attendant's
mandatory smile, the TAM staying warm and composed through a hostile escalation.
Two strategies:

- **Surface acting**: *faking* the required emotion while feeling something else.
  You display calm warmth you don't feel. Creates **emotional dissonance** (felt vs
  shown), and reliably predicts **emotional exhaustion, lower job satisfaction,
  poorer service performance, and burnout.** It's the costly one.
- **Deep acting**: actually *working yourself into* the required feeling (via
  reappraisal, perspective-taking, attention) so the display is genuine. Generally
  **does not** carry surface acting's costs; can even support job satisfaction and a
  sense of accomplishment. (Caveat: emotional labor of *any* kind has some baseline
  cost; deep acting is less harmful, not free.)

**The mechanism is just §3 applied to display rules:** deep acting = antecedent
reappraisal of how you genuinely feel; surface acting = response-focused suppression
+ a faked display. The regulation literature's verdict (reappraise > suppress) is
*why* deep acting beats surface acting.

**Operator moves:**
- **Choose deep acting on hard calls.** Before a hostile escalation, don't just
  paste on a smile (surface acting → dissonance → exhaustion). Genuinely reappraise:
  "this customer's anger is fear about their job; I'd be scared too." Now the
  composure you show is *real*, and it costs you far less.
- **Notice chronic surface acting as an early-warning sign.** Days of faking
  composure you don't feel is the documented on-ramp to **burnout**. That syndrome —
  exhaustion/cynicism/inefficacy, Job Demands-Resources, recovery, detachment — is
  owned by `performance-and-resilience-psychology`. The bridge is here: *surface
  acting is one of the demands that feeds it.* Cross over when faking-feeling
  becomes chronic.

## Integrated playbook: the heated-customer escalation

A single scenario tying the mechanisms together. A key stakeholder is furious on a
call — an outage hit them, they're talking about churning, voice raised.

1. **Regulate yourself first (contagion + regulation).** You *will* catch their
   arousal (§6). Before reacting: one slow breath (response modulation as a bridge,
   §3), and reappraise — "this is fear about their standing, aimed at the situation,
   not a verdict on me" (§1, §3). This is **deep acting** (§8): you generate real
   composure, not a fake one.
2. **Drop their arousal before solving (dimensional frame).** They're high-arousal,
   negative-valence (§2). Do **not** lead with the fix — they can't process it. Give
   room to vent; slow your pace and lower your tone (you're now a calm contagion
   *source*, co-regulating them down, §6). Acknowledge the feeling for *rapport* —
   "I can hear how serious this is for you" — without assuming a forced label tames
   it (§3 naming caveat).
3. **Re-appraise their event toward challenge + coping (appraisal).** Once arousal
   drops: move the primary appraisal threat→challenge and the secondary appraisal
   low→high coping — "this is a bad day, *and* it's a known issue with a clear fix,
   here's the plan and the owner" (§1). You're changing what the outage *means*.
4. **De-focalize their forecast (affective forecasting).** If they're catastrophizing
   ("this ruins everything"), gently widen the time frame: "what'll matter in a month
   is how we respond — here's that response" (§5). Reframe durability, don't dismiss.
5. **Mind your own judgment afterward (affect-as-information).** Right after a brutal
   call, don't make a consequential call on *another* account — your negative mood
   will bleed into it (§4). Name the source; defer the judgment.
6. **Audit your labor over time (emotional labor → burnout bridge).** If every call
   is surface acting, that's the burnout on-ramp — hand off to
   `performance-and-resilience-psychology` (§8).

## Anti-patterns

- **Reading true feelings off faces.** Basic-emotion "microexpression" reads are
  weak in real life (§2). Verify with words; treat expression as a soft cue.
- **"Always reappraise."** Reappraising a problem you could *fix* is maladaptive
  (Troy 2013, §3). If you control the stressor, change the situation; reserve
  reappraisal for what you can't control.
- **Bare suppression as your main tool.** White-knuckling a neutral face raises your
  own physiological load, hurts your memory of the call, and *reduces* rapport (§3).
  Use deep acting, not raw surface suppression (§8).
- **"Name it to tame it" as a reflex on yourself.** Acknowledging a *customer's*
  emotion builds rapport; forcing a precise label on *your own* feeling can
  *crystallize* it and make reappraisal harder (Nook 2021, §3).
- **Treating EQ as destiny.** "High EQ → success" overshoots the data; EI's validity
  is modest and near-zero once IQ + Big Five are controlled (§7).
- **Solving before de-escalating.** Pitching the fix to a high-arousal customer
  wastes it — they can't process until arousal drops (§2, integrated playbook).
- **Ignoring contagion direction.** Letting one panicked voice set a war-room's
  emotional baseline; forgetting that *you* infect the room too (§6).
- **Judging in a borrowed mood.** Making an evaluative call while a strong unrelated
  mood is active, without naming the source (§4).

mechanism-grounded plan for a charged interaction.

manager. Ground every recommendation in these mechanisms and name which one
you're using: Lazarus/Scherer APPRAISAL (primary=threat/challenge,

valence); GROSS emotion regulation (situation selection/modification, attentional

de-focalize catastrophizing); EMOTIONAL CONTAGION (you catch and radiate
arousal — co-regulate); EMOTIONAL LABOR (choose deep acting over surface acting).

Honesty rules: do NOT claim you can read true feelings off a face; do NOT tell me
to "always reappraise"; do NOT oversell "name it to tame it" for my own feelings;

- Situation: <what happened, channel, who's involved>
- Customer's apparent state: <words used, tone, valence + arousal if you can tell>
- My current state: <am I already activated / have I caught their arousal?>
- What I control vs don't: <which parts of the problem are fixable by me>
- Goal for this interaction: <de-escalate / decide / preserve relationship / …>

OUTPUT
1. Read of the customer's likely APPRAISAL (primary threat-vs-challenge; secondary
   coping high-vs-low) and the probable emotion driver (anger=other-blame;
   anxiety=low control).
2. My self-regulation step BEFORE I respond (contagion check + which Gross family;
   deep-acting reappraisal script).
3. Arousal-down sequence before any solution (concrete words + vocal/pace guidance).
4. Appraisal-shift script (threat→challenge, low→high coping) — without denying the
   problem.
5. If they're catastrophizing: a de-focalizing reframe of DURABILITY (not a dismissal).
6. Whether I should fix-the-situation vs reappraise (per controllability) — be explicit.
7. A one-line caution about judging other accounts in the mood this leaves me in.
8. Flag if this looks like chronic surface acting (→ burnout risk; recommend the
   resilience hand-off).
Keep it tight and immediately usable on a live call.
