# Academic and Citation Writing

Academic writing is argument with receipts. Every non-trivial claim ties to a source the reader can independently retrieve and verify.

## Core Concepts

### 1. The five major citation systems at a glance

| Style | In-text format | Reference list | Field |
|---|---|---|---|
| **APA 7th** | (Smith, 2024, p. 42) | References — author-date | Social sciences, psychology, education, nursing |
| **Chicago 17th — Author-Date** | (Smith 2024, 42) | References — author-date | Sciences, social sciences |
| **Chicago 17th — Notes-Bibliography** | ¹ footnote | Bibliography — alphabetical | Humanities, history, literature, arts |
| **MLA 9th** | (Smith 42) | Works Cited — alphabetical | Languages, literature, humanities |
| **IEEE** | [1] | References — numeric | Engineering, computer science |
| **Vancouver** | (1) or superscript ¹ | References — numeric | Biomedical, medical |

### 2. APA 7th edition — author-date system

- **Parenthetical:** "Working memory degrades under cognitive load (Smith, 2024)."
- **Narrative:** "Smith (2024) found that working memory degrades under cognitive load."

Direct quotes require a page or paragraph number: "(Smith, 2024, p. 42)"

### 3. Signal phrases — integrating sources into prose

Signal-phrase verbs carry stance:
- **Neutral:** notes, states, reports, observes, describes, writes
- **Argumentative:** argues, contends, claims, asserts, maintains
- **Evidence-based:** found, demonstrated, showed, established, documented
- **Hedged:** suggests, proposes, hypothesizes, speculates, implies
- **Critical:** disputes, challenges, questions, complicates, refutes

### 4. Pseudo-citation — the failure mode

**"Studies show..."** with no citation is pseudo-citation. Three repairs:
1. **Name the source.** "Studies show" → "Cowan (2010) and Baddeley (2012) showed".
2. **Drop the unsupportable claim.** If you cannot find a citation, you do not yet have evidence.
3. **Mark it as your own argument.** "I argue that..." is honest.

### 5. Primary, secondary, tertiary sources

- **Primary** — original research, raw data, firsthand accounts.
- **Secondary** — analysis or interpretation of primary sources.
- **Tertiary** — compilations of secondary sources. Wikipedia, encyclopedias.

### 6. Persistent identifiers

- **DOI (Digital Object Identifier)** — Format `10.xxxx/yyyy`. Include as `https://doi.org/10.xxxx/yyyy`. Required by APA 7, IEEE.
- **ORCID** — persistent identifier for an author.
- **arXiv ID** — preprints in physics, CS, math.

### 7. Toulmin argument structure

1. **Claim** — the conclusion you want the reader to accept.
2. **Grounds** (data, evidence) — the facts that support the claim.
3. **Warrant** — the underlying principle that lets the grounds support the claim.
4. **Backing** — support for the warrant itself.
5. **Qualifier** — limits on the claim.
6. **Rebuttal** — counterclaims and exceptions acknowledged.

### 8. The literature-review funnel

1. **Field opening** — the broad domain
2. **Sub-area** — the specific corner of the field
3. **Active debate** — the specific scholarly conversation
4. **The gap** — what is missing
5. **Your contribution** — what your work does

### 9. Abstract vs. introduction — different jobs

**Abstract** — a self-contained summary readable without the paper. 150–300 words.
- Context / problem (1 sentence)
- Method (1–2 sentences)
- Results (2–3 sentences with the actual numbers)
- Implication (1 sentence)

**Introduction** — the opening section of the paper proper. Reviews relevant prior work, identifies the gap, states the research question.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **Pseudo-citation** — "Studies show...", "research suggests..."
2. **Quote-bombing** — paragraph after paragraph of standalone quotations
3. **Secondary cited as primary** — citing a source without reading it
4. **Style mixing** — APA in-text with MLA works-cited entries
5. **Missing DOIs** — references with URLs that will rot
