# Profile Writing

## Overview

A journalistic profile is not a biography, a hagiography, a resume in prose, or a Q&A. It is a **narrative reported piece** that uses reporting, scene, anecdote, and selected detail to reveal something true about a person.

Three pieces of writing define the modern profile tradition: Gay Talese's "Frank Sinatra Has a Cold" (Esquire, 1966) for scene-based reporting; Janet Malcolm's "The Journalist and the Murderer" (The New Yorker, 1989) for the ethics of the form; and Walt Harrington's work in *Intimate Journalism* — the discipline of "deep, not wide."

## Core Concepts

### 1. The profile is not the subject's biography

The biographer's question: what happened? The profile writer's question: **what is this person actually like, and how do I show it?**

### 2. The five-part structure

1. **Lead (lede).** A scene, anecdote, or paradox that introduces the subject in a specific moment. 1–3 paragraphs.
2. **Nut graph (nutgraf).** The "why this story now" paragraph. 1 paragraph.
3. **Context / background.** What the reader needs to know about the world the subject operates in. 2–4 paragraphs.
4. **Biography and present moment.** Selected biographical material, interleaved with scenes from current reporting.
5. **Kicker.** The closing scene or quote. Often a callback to the lede.

### 3. Lede patterns

**In-scene lede.** Open inside a moment. The subject is doing something.

**Anecdotal lede.** Open with a story — a discrete past event that illustrates something about the subject.

**Paradoxical lede.** Open with the contradiction at the heart of the subject.

What never works: opening with the subject's birth, their resume, or their official title.

### 4. The nutgraf and its placement

The "nut graph" tells the reader what the piece is about and why they should keep reading. It answers:
- Who is this person? (One-sentence positioning.)
- Why now? (What just happened that makes them newsworthy.)
- What does this piece argue or reveal?

The nut comes early enough that the reader doesn't bail, late enough that the lede has done its work.

### 5. Show in scene, summarize in transit

Walt Harrington's craft rule: **render the important moments as scenes; summarize everything in between.**

Pick 3–5 scenes that do narrative work. Render those in close, sensory, present-tense detail. Everything else is summary prose.

**Scene → summary → scene → summary → scene → kicker.**

### 6. The interview-to-prose distillation

The discipline:
- **Quote sparingly.** Direct quotes should do work no paraphrase could do — voice, idiosyncrasy, surprise, contradiction.
- **Never start a paragraph with a long quote.**
- **Action over speech.** What the subject *does* in your presence beats what they *say* about themselves.

### 7. The reporting requirement

Minimum table stakes for a serious profile:
- 1–3 long-form interviews with the subject (90+ minutes each).
- 5–15 interviews with people who know the subject.
- Direct observation of the subject in their normal environment.
- Document review.

### 8. The ethical posture (Janet Malcolm)

No deception about the assignment. No fabricated composites. No invented dialogue. No quote-clean-up that changes meaning. No quote approval. No surprises in the final piece — give subjects the chance to address material discoveries.

### 9. Fact-checking discipline

Tape every interview. Keep a fact log: every claim → source. For claims about people other than the subject, contact those people for confirmation or comment.

### 10. The kicker

Three reliable kicker patterns:
1. **Callback.** The lede scene returns, but the reader now understands it differently.
2. **Crystallizing quote.** A single line from the subject that compresses the piece's argument.
3. **Forward-look.** A small concrete detail of the subject's near future.

## Profile skeleton (≈3,500-word feature)

```
[Lede — 200-400 words]
  Scene or anecdote. Subject in motion. Sensory detail.

[Nutgraf — 100-200 words]
  Who. Why now. What this piece reveals.

[Context — 300-500 words]
  The world the subject operates in.

[First biographical pass — 400-700 words]

[Scene 2 — 300-500 words]

[Second biographical pass — 400-700 words]

[Tension / complication — 400-600 words]
  The contradiction, the critic, the unresolved thing.

[Scene 3 — 300-500 words]

[Kicker — 100-300 words]
  Callback or crystallizing image.
```

## Anti-Patterns

- **The hagiography.** Profile reads as press release.
- **The resume in prose.** Chronological recitation with no scene.
- **The Q&A in disguise.** Long stretches of question-answer.
- **The writer in the foreground.** "When I first met Smith, I expected..."
- **The unattributed psychoanalysis.** "Smith is a deeply lonely man." On whose authority?
- **Composite characters / invented dialogue.** Fabrication.
